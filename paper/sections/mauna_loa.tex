\section{Observed-data illustration: atmospheric carbon dioxide}
We update the Mauna Loa atmospheric $\mathrm{CO}_2$ illustration
through the most recently released complete monthly observation
available on October 25, 2026. The NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory
(GML) monthly means cover January 1990--September 2026,
$n=441$ equally spaced months; the September 2026 mean is
$426.07$ parts per million (ppm) \cite{noaa2026mlo}.
The October 2026 monthly mean was not available at the
data cutoff. We analyze the \emph{published monthly means},
without estimating missing observations or removing the
substantial annual seasonal fluctuations.

These data differ in provenance from the archived 1990--1999
example, which aggregated the historical weekly series distributed
by \texttt{statsmodels.datasets.co2}. The original
120-month data, figure, and reproduction script remain in
the repository, but the updated plot uses NOAA's monthly
averages directly. Values for 1990--August 2026 were
transcribed from a public mirror of NOAA's monthly series
and the September 2026 value was checked against NOAA's
October 9 release. The exact 441-value slice, source locations,
and cutoff date are recorded in
\texttt{figures/noaa\_mlo\_monthly\_1990\_2026\_09.csv}.
NOAA values are subject to subsequent revision.

As in the archived illustration, we fix the reference
difference mean at $\mu=0$, prescribe the same normalized
conditional trace smoothness $S=0.86$, and compare orders
$d\in\{2,3,4,5\}$. For each order we invert
Eq.~\eqref{eq:normalized-scale}, then fit the trend
with the recovered penalty. The new penalties
$\lambda$ are, respectively, approximately
$41.77$, $175.72$, $787.53$, and $3564.46$.
The associated conditional effective degrees of freedom,
$\nu=n-(n-d)S$, are $63.46$, $64.32$, $65.18$,
and $66.04$. Unlike a common numeric penalty,
a common $S$ controls the \emph{fraction of penalizable
trace degrees of freedom} across orders;
it does not guarantee identical geometric trends.

The observed rising CO$_2$ baseline and seasonal
oscillations illustrate why a smoothed historical trend is
not automatically an estimate of measurement noise or a
seasonally adjusted signal. Different orders preserve
different polynomial null spaces and can behave
differently near the endpoints. Using the full historical
series gives a \emph{two-sided} trend estimate: observations
later than a given month enter that month's fitted value.
This comparison does not estimate $\mu$ from observations or optimize
a Gaussian likelihood; no held-out future period or forecast accuracy
is evaluated. The displayed two-sided fitted curves are not forecasts.

A substantive atmospheric analysis would explicitly model
annual seasonality, site changes, measurement uncertainty,
and later data revisions. These tasks, like statistical
inference for a jointly estimated $(\lambda,\mu)$, remain
outside the present computational comparison.
