Abstract
Selecting the smoothing parameter of a finite-difference penalized least-squares
estimator requires evaluation of a statistical selection criterion, not merely
solution of a penalized linear system. We examine the computational advan-
tages and numerical limitations of reusing a singular-value decomposition (SVD)
across candidate parameters, compared with a structured banded-Cholesky
implementation. The study covers 29 prespecified configurations, 97 random-
seed realizations per configuration, multiple selection criteria and one- versus
multiple-candidate workloads. Valid execution times are matched by realization,
workload and execution phase, with uncertainty estimated by seed-cluster boot-
strap. For an order-three conditional cross-validation problem withn= 256, the
banded/SVD complete cold-time ratio is 0.698 (95% interval 0.671–0.724) for a
single candidate but 4.846 (4.567–5.125) for a 100-candidate grid. In nine config-
urations, the banded complete-grid implementation fails its post-solve optimality
check for all recorded grid evaluations while the spectral pipeline completes them;
both methods exhibit smoothness-inversion failures in larger fourth-order con-
figurations. Dense spectral storage nevertheless scales quadratically with series
length. The results support spectral decomposition as the preferred implemen-
tation for repeated complete-criterion searches when memory permits, while
retaining banded methods for isolated evaluations and memory-constrained
problems.

1 Introduction
Penalized least-squares smoothing is a widely used approach to separating low-
frequency variation from noise in an observed series. Since the work of Whittaker
Whittaker (1923), finite-difference penalties have offered a direct connection between
trend regularity and linear smoothing. Subsequent developments include structured
implementations of Whittaker–Henderson smoothing Weinert (2007, 2012); Biessy
(2026) and the interpretation of smoothing through the percentage-of-smoothness
parameter introduced by Guerrero Guerrero (2007, 2008). Selection of the penalty by
cross-validation, information criteria or marginal likelihood is well established Craven
and Wahba (1979); Cort´ es-Toto et al. (2017). Nevertheless, obtaining a fitted trend
and evaluating thecomplete selection criterionare computationally different tasks.
A banded Cholesky factorization exploits the sparsity of finite-difference penal-
ties and is economical for an individual smoothing parameter. In contrast, a spectral
factorization requires an appreciable initial calculation but exposes all candidate penal-
ties through diagonal transformations in a common basis. Consequently, the apparent
superiority of a solver in a single fit need not persist across a grid of smoothing param-
eters. Moreover, numerical acceptance of the complete criterion depends not only on
the matrix factorization but also on leverage calculations, endpoint treatment and the
inversion between penalty and smoothness coordinates.
We investigate the following practical question:When is a spectral implementa-
tion preferable for selecting a smoothing parameter by a complete statistical criterion?
We compare measured end-to-end costs of spectral singular-value decomposition
(SVD), banded Cholesky and available reference implementations under paired com-
putational workloads. Unlike a fit-only benchmark, the comparisons charge setup,
coordinate conversion, criterion evaluation and diagnostics to the method that incurs
them. Unsuccessful or incomplete searches remain observable outcomes rather than
being treated as fast computations. This permits a recommendation based jointly on
execution time, observed reliability and storage requirements.
Our main conclusion is that spectral decomposition is an effectivedefault choice for
repeated, complete-criterion searches at feasible matrix sizes. That recommendation
is empirical and conditional: structured banded computation remains competitive for
isolated candidate evaluations and retains important storage advantages for long series.
We do not propose a new PLS estimator, nor do we interpret computation time as
predictive accuracy.

3 Computational study
3.1 Experimental factors and execution environment
The study evaluates 29 prespecified computational configurations and 97 independent
random-seed realizations per configuration, giving 2813 scheduled case–seed jobs. Con-
figurations vary series lengthn, difference orderd, criterion, reference mean, signal
amplitude, and grid densityM. Four workload types are considered: one candidate
for one series,Mcandidates for one series, one candidate forRseries, andMcan-
didates forRseries. The tested grid isS j =j/Mforj= 1, . . . , M, including its
analytic endpoint. Selected configurations useM= 100, 1000, or 10000; the examined
sample lengths includen= 64, 128, 256, 512, 1024, 2048, and 4096. Comparisons use
identical simulated series and evaluation coordinates when both implementations are
admissible.
The computations were performed on a Windows x64 multicore workstation, with
one BLAS thread per job. The execution schedule comprised phases allowing at most
24, 1, or 12 concurrent jobs, with 2231, 52, and 530 jobs, respectively. These limits do
not measure instantaneous CPU occupancy. We therefore retain the execution phase
as a matching variable and avoid interpreting multi-job wall-clock times as contention-
free single-process latency. Within a case–seed, method ordering was rotated and
repeated where prescribed.
Two coordinate-evaluation policies were implemented: directly invertingSfor each
requested candidate and converting the fullSgrid toλonce for reuse. Both policies
target thesamesmoothing values. The reported complete cold time includes method
initialization, smoothness-to-penalty conversion and all candidate criterion evalua-
tions; recorded warm accounting excludes the one-time initialization term but does
not represent a separately launched process.
3.2 Complete-search validity and paired comparisons
Every scheduled job was reconciled with its saved numerical and timing outcomes. A
timing row is admitted only when the process terminates successfully, the selected score
and elapsed time are finite, the recorded status is successful, and allMRprescribed
evaluations are present. Missing files, rejected numerical checks, inaccurate smooth-
ness inversions and resource-limit exclusions remain in the denominator of descriptive
reliability summaries. Successful evaluation of only one grid point is never substituted
for completion of the full grid.
For paired comparisons, methods must additionally agree on selected smooth-
ness within 10 −10 and, for directly comparable non-likelihood scores, on the selected
criterion value within relative tolerance 10 −7. These criteria assess agreement
between available double-precision implementations, not uniform high-precision accu-
racy throughout the parameter grid. The implementation and machine configuration,
execution phase, realization, coordinate policy, workload and repeat are matched.

4.3 Reuse across smoothing coordinates and series
The candidate representation also influences total cost. Two computational policies
were compared: repeated conversion of smoothness valuesSto the penaltyλ, and
a single conversion shared across the search. The candidate set is identical under
both policies, so the contrast concerns computational reuse rather than a different
optimization problem.
For spectral CV withn= 256,d= 3, 100 candidates and three series, the geomet-
ric complete cold-time ratio of once-mapped penalties to repeated direct-Sconversion
is 0.580 (95% interval 0.570–0.590). In this setting, precomputing the candidate trans-
formation reduces total execution time. Figure 3 displays corresponding descriptive
comparisons across other numerically admissible multi-series grid configurations.
Figure 4 illustrates how a fixed first-order GCV configuration responds to four
combinations of candidate and series count. The comparisons show why initialization,
single-candidate scoring, multiple-series reuse and full-grid selection must be distin-
guished when assessing an implementation. In particular, a procedure optimized only
for one fitted trend need not minimize the cost of selecting a smoothing parameter.

4.5 Post-solve optimality failures in banded computation
The banded-Cholesky implementation reported 9096 method-level timing observa-
tions rejected by the relative optimality-residual check in Eq. (11). The recorded
residuals range from approximately 1.00×10−7 to 1.06×10 −6, compared with the pre-
scribed tolerance 10 −7. Since the check is performed after factorization and solution,
these observations do not establish the breakdown of Cholesky factorization itself.
They identify loss of admissibility in thecomplete banded criterion pipelineunder its
implemented numerical diagnostic.
A particularly informative contrast occurs in nine parameter configurations. For
each configuration, 97 independent realizations were considered. Both methods com-
pleted every recorded single-candidate evaluation, but the banded implementation
rejected every complete-grid evaluation, whereas the spectral implementation com-
pleted the corresponding grids. The configurations comprise CV and GCV with
(n, d, M) = (128,3,1000), (256,4,100) and (256,3,1000); GCV with (64,3,10000);
and profiled Gaussian marginal likelihood with (256,3,100) and (512,3,100). Figure 5
displays these results.
The nine configurations account for 6984 banded-rejection timing records; another
2112 such records arise from larger fourth-order configurations. These are repeated
method-level observations within 97 independent seed clusters per configuration,
rather than thousands of independent experimental realizations. The saved status
information does not identify the exact smoothing-grid point that first produced each
rejection, and hence no candidate-specific instability boundary is inferred.
The apparent spectral advantage must be interpreted asobserved computational
completion, not a controlled comparison of identical backward-error tests. The spectral
scorer does not impose the same implementation-specific acceptance criterion as the
banded scorer. Independent numerical reference calculations would be required to
establish a strict relative-accuracy ranking at each penalty.

4.6 Smoothness inversion remains a common numerical
limitation
An additional failure mechanism affects both implementations at larger difference
order and series length. In the fourth-order CV/GCV configurations withn= 1024,
2048 and 4096, the numerical inversion of the normalized smoothness mapping
sometimes returned values outside its prescribed tolerance. Across the evaluated con-
figurations, 6336 timing records explicitly describe inaccurate smoothness inversion:
2112 associated with the banded method and 4224 with SVD. The distribution of
observed outcomes in the fourth-order settings is given in Fig. 6.
This error concerns the transformation from the bounded coordinateSto the
penaltyλ, rather than evidence that a spectral decomposition failed. Crucially, a
successfully completed one-candidate evaluation cannot be used as a substitute for an
incomplete multi-candidate search. Accordingly, the large-order configurations with
shared inversion difficulties do not supply an empirical winner for the full criterion
grid.

4.8 Memory requirements and computational feasibility
The principal disadvantage of the spectral approach is its dense storage requirement.
For the evaluated implementation, the conservative memory admission allowance cor-
responds to fiven×ndouble-precision arrays, or 40n 2 bytes. An explicit dense-inverse
reference is assigned seven such arrays, or 56n2 bytes. In contrast, a banded Cholesky
factor and a banded selected-inverse buffer together require approximately 16n(d+ 1)
bytes, excluding ancillary data structures. Figure 8 illustrates these storage relations
ford= 4.
For example, the spectral admission allowance is 640 MiB atn= 4096. Atn= 8192
it rises to 2.5 GiB, exceeding the 2 GiB dense allocation limit imposed in the study.
The latter sample length was not evaluated in the present comparison. The curves
therefore representimplementation-specific memory allowances and illustrative buffer
sizes, not measurements of peak resident memory or empirical allocation failures.
There were 970 resource-budget exclusions in the recorded timing outcomes: 776
for the explicit-inverse reference and 194 for SVD. These were induced by prescribed
computational-work thresholds, not observed out-of-memory errors. Thus the mem-
ory analysis establishes a concrete feasibility trade-off without attributing numerical
failures to unsupported memory measurements.

recommended default for finite-difference PLS smoothing-parameter selection over
a moderate-to-large candidate grid. In contrast to a sequence of penalized normal-
equation factorizations, spectral preprocessing represents the complete search in one
orthogonal basis. It amortizes setup, supplies degrees of freedom and residual sums of

The recommendation should not be extended beyond the observed evidence. First,
the banded method is often preferable when evaluating only one penalty. Second, the
spectral basis and its work arrays require quadratic memory, which can dominate the
decision for long series even when SVD is attractive at moderaten. Third, numerical
acceptance is not interchangeable with certified forward accuracy: the two implemen-
tations apply different diagnostic checks, and both encounter smoothness-inversion
difficulties in the larger fourth-order cases. Fourth, the recorded multi-process exe-
cution phases limit the transportability of wall-clock ratios to idle workstations or
different parallel workloads.
These limitations suggest the following practical selection principle. If the task is
a multi-candidatecomplete-criterionsearch and the dense spectral workspace satis-
fies a conservative memory budget, prepare the spectral decomposition once, reuse
series-specific projections and precompute the candidate penalties when appropriate.
If only one candidate is needed, or if dense allocation would be prohibitive, a struc-
tured banded method is a reasonable alternative subject to numerical residual checks.
When a requested smoothness value cannot be inverted accurately, neither method
should silently substitute an approximate grid winner: the affected comparison must
be marked invalid or handled by a separately validated numerical strategy.
14
A further limitation is the restriction to synthetic data and one computational
environment. Seed-cluster intervals quantify variability under the observed experimen-
tal protocol, but not variability across operating systems, processors, library versions
or CPU utilization. The validity masks can induce survivor bias in timing ratios
conditional on completed searches. The study does not establish improvements in
trend-estimation risk or forecasting accuracy, and it does not constitute an indepen-
dent high-precision verification of every evaluated penalty. Future work should isolate
the source of the large-order smoothness-inversion failures, independently certify resid-
ual and criterion accuracy, and compare implementations under controlled processor
occupancy.
6 Conclusions
This study establishes that the preferred computational method for penalized least-
squares trend smoothing depends on theparameter-selection workload, not merely the
cost of one linear solve. Across valid matched 100-candidate CV and GCV searches,
spectral SVD consistently outperformed banded Cholesky in the representative tested
configurations. A measured conditional-CV setting changed from favoring banded
Cholesky for one candidate to favoring spectral SVD by a factor of approximately 4.85
for the complete grid. The numerical-status analysis further identified repeated banded
post-solve failures on several complete grids, while retaining documented failure modes
of the spectral pipeline.
We therefore recommendspectral decomposition as the primary implementation for
complete, repeated smoothing-parameter selection whenever its dense storage is afford-
able. Banded methods remain valuable for isolated evaluations and long series, and
numerical diagnostics remain mandatory for either implementation. The contribution
is a measured, criterion-aware recommendation for computational practice rather than
a new trend estimator or an unconditional claim of spectral superiority.
