Abstract
Selecting the smoothing parameter of a finite-difference penalized least-squares
estimator requires evaluation of a statistical selection criterion, not merely
solution of a penalized linear system. We examine the computational advantages and numerical limitations of reusing a singular-value decomposition (SVD)
across candidate parameters, compared with a structured banded-Cholesky
implementation. The study covers 29 prespecified configurations, 97 randomseed realizations per configuration, multiple selection criteria and one- versus
multiple-candidate workloads. Valid execution times are matched by realization,
workload and execution phase, with uncertainty estimated by seed-cluster bootstrap. For an order-three conditional cross-validation problem with n = 256, the
banded/SVD complete cold-time ratio is 0.698 (95% interval 0.671–0.724) for a
single candidate but 4.846 (4.567–5.125) for a 100-candidate grid. In nine configurations, the banded complete-grid implementation fails its post-solve optimality
check for all recorded grid evaluations while the spectral pipeline completes them;
both methods exhibit smoothness-inversion failures in larger fourth-order configurations. Dense spectral storage nevertheless scales quadratically with series
length. The results support spectral decomposition as the preferred implementation for repeated complete-criterion searches when memory permits, while
retaining banded methods for isolated evaluations and memory-constrained
problems.
Keywords: penalized least squares, smoothing-parameter selection, singular-value
decomposition, Whittaker–Henderson smoothing, cross-validation, numerical
reliability, computational performance

1 Introduction
Penalized least-squares smoothing is a widely used approach to separating lowfrequency variation from noise in an observed series. Since the work of Whittaker
Whittaker (1923), finite-difference penalties have offered a direct connection between
trend regularity and linear smoothing. Subsequent developments include structured
implementations of Whittaker–Henderson smoothing Weinert (2007, 2012); Biessy
(2026) and the interpretation of smoothing through the percentage-of-smoothness
parameter introduced by Guerrero Guerrero (2007, 2008). Selection of the penalty by
cross-validation, information criteria or marginal likelihood is well established Craven
and Wahba (1979); Cortés-Toto et al. (2017). Nevertheless, obtaining a fitted trend
and evaluating the complete selection criterion are computationally different tasks.
A banded Cholesky factorization exploits the sparsity of finite-difference penalties and is economical for an individual smoothing parameter. In contrast, a spectral
factorization requires an appreciable initial calculation but exposes all candidate penalties through diagonal transformations in a common basis. Consequently, the apparent
superiority of a solver in a single fit need not persist across a grid of smoothing parameters. Moreover, numerical acceptance of the complete criterion depends not only on
the matrix factorization but also on leverage calculations, endpoint treatment and the
inversion between penalty and smoothness coordinates.
We investigate the following practical question: When is a spectral implementation preferable for selecting a smoothing parameter by a complete statistical criterion?
We compare measured end-to-end costs of spectral singular-value decomposition
(SVD), banded Cholesky and available reference implementations under paired computational workloads. Unlike a fit-only benchmark, the comparisons charge setup,
coordinate conversion, criterion evaluation and diagnostics to the method that incurs
them. Unsuccessful or incomplete searches remain observable outcomes rather than
being treated as fast computations. This permits a recommendation based jointly on
execution time, observed reliability and storage requirements.
Our main conclusion is that spectral decomposition is an effective default choice for
repeated, complete-criterion searches at feasible matrix sizes. That recommendation
is empirical and conditional: structured banded computation remains competitive for
isolated candidate evaluations and retains important storage advantages for long series.
We do not propose a new PLS estimator, nor do we interpret computation time as
predictive accuracy.

2.4 Banded evaluation and numerical admissibility
The banded method factorizes Aλ = I + λDd⊤ Dd for individual penalties. A selectedinverse recursion supplies the diagonal leverage values and effective degrees of freedom
needed by the complete criteria, without forming a dense inverse. For fixed low difference order, the banded factor and selected inverse have storage proportional to
nd, whereas the full spectral basis requires storage proportional to n2 . An explicit
dense-inverse method and an available sparse-factorization/structured-diagnostics
hybrid serve only as additional comparison procedures; the latter is not treated as a
sparse-only score evaluator.
A numerical completion requirement is especially important for the banded procedure. After factorization and solution of the normal equations, the implementation

3 Computational study
3.1 Experimental factors and execution environment
The study evaluates 29 prespecified computational configurations and 97 independent
random-seed realizations per configuration, giving 2813 scheduled case–seed jobs. Configurations vary series length n, difference order d, criterion, reference mean, signal
amplitude, and grid density M . Four workload types are considered: one candidate
for one series, M candidates for one series, one candidate for R series, and M candidates for R series. The tested grid is Sj = j/M for j = 1, . . . , M , including its
analytic endpoint. Selected configurations use M = 100, 1000, or 10000; the examined
sample lengths include n = 64, 128, 256, 512, 1024, 2048, and 4096. Comparisons use
identical simulated series and evaluation coordinates when both implementations are
admissible.
The computations were performed on a Windows x64 multicore workstation, with
one BLAS thread per job. The execution schedule comprised phases allowing at most
24, 1, or 12 concurrent jobs, with 2231, 52, and 530 jobs, respectively. These limits do
not measure instantaneous CPU occupancy. We therefore retain the execution phase
as a matching variable and avoid interpreting multi-job wall-clock times as contentionfree single-process latency. Within a case–seed, method ordering was rotated and
repeated where prescribed.
Two coordinate-evaluation policies were implemented: directly inverting S for each
requested candidate and converting the full S grid to λ once for reuse. Both policies
target the same smoothing values. The reported complete cold time includes method
initialization, smoothness-to-penalty conversion and all candidate criterion evaluations; recorded warm accounting excludes the one-time initialization term but does
not represent a separately launched process.

3.2 Complete-search validity and paired comparisons
Every scheduled job was reconciled with its saved numerical and timing outcomes. A
timing row is admitted only when the process terminates successfully, the selected score
and elapsed time are finite, the recorded status is successful, and all M R prescribed
evaluations are present. Missing files, rejected numerical checks, inaccurate smoothness inversions and resource-limit exclusions remain in the denominator of descriptive
reliability summaries. Successful evaluation of only one grid point is never substituted
for completion of the full grid.
For paired comparisons, methods must additionally agree on selected smoothness within 10−10 and, for directly comparable non-likelihood scores, on the selected
criterion value within relative tolerance 10−7 . These criteria assess agreement
between available double-precision implementations, not uniform high-precision accuracy throughout the parameter grid. The implementation and machine configuration,
execution phase, realization, coordinate policy, workload and repeat are matched.

4 Empirical results
4.1 Completion rates and admissible observations
Of 2813 scheduled computational jobs, 57 terminated without a successful numerical record. The resulting dataset comprises 92,679 measured or explicitly classified
method-level outcomes. A total of 76,220 (82.24%) satisfy the predefined completeness and status conditions. The remaining 16,459 comprise 11,208 numerical-rejection
records, 4224 additional smoothness-inversion failure records, 970 computationalbudget exclusions, and 57 records representing unavailable job inputs. The classification retains unsuccessful searches; omitting them would bias subsequent comparisons
toward implementations with a higher apparent completion rate.
Figure 1 summarizes status-admissible proportions for the evaluated criteria and
implementation families. Because the methods were not all attempted in the same
parameter configurations, aggregate percentages should be interpreted descriptively
rather than as probabilities from a randomized, identically distributed reliability trial.

4.3 Reuse across smoothing coordinates and series
The candidate representation also influences total cost. Two computational policies
were compared: repeated conversion of smoothness values S to the penalty λ, and
a single conversion shared across the search. The candidate set is identical under
both policies, so the contrast concerns computational reuse rather than a different
optimization problem.
For spectral CV with n = 256, d = 3, 100 candidates and three series, the geometric complete cold-time ratio of once-mapped penalties to repeated direct-S conversion
is 0.580 (95% interval 0.570–0.590). In this setting, precomputing the candidate transformation reduces total execution time. Figure 3 displays corresponding descriptive
comparisons across other numerically admissible multi-series grid configurations.
Figure 4 illustrates how a fixed first-order GCV configuration responds to four
combinations of candidate and series count. The comparisons show why initialization,
single-candidate scoring, multiple-series reuse and full-grid selection must be distinguished when assessing an implementation. In particular, a procedure optimized only
for one fitted trend need not minimize the cost of selecting a smoothing parameter.

4.4 Numerical reliability and its consequences
The aggregate status proportions in the 24-worker execution phase are 10,848/13,176
(82.3%) for banded CV and 13,176/13,176 (100%) for spectral CV. The corresponding GCV counts are 13,176/16,280 (80.9%) and 15,892/16,086 (98.8%), respectively.
Their denominators differ because method eligibility and the evaluated configurations
differ; they should not be used as an unconditional estimate of solver accuracy. The
comparison nevertheless motivates a closer examination of the failure mechanisms,
reported next.

4.5 Post-solve optimality failures in banded computation

residuals range from approximately 1.00 × 10−7 to 1.06 × 10−6 , compared with the prescribed tolerance 10−7 . Since the check is performed after factorization and solution,
these observations do not establish the breakdown of Cholesky factorization itself.
They identify loss of admissibility in the complete banded criterion pipeline under its
implemented numerical diagnostic.

4.8 Memory requirements and computational feasibility
The principal disadvantage of the spectral approach is its dense storage requirement.
For the evaluated implementation, the conservative memory admission allowance corresponds to five n × n double-precision arrays, or 40n2 bytes. An explicit dense-inverse
reference is assigned seven such arrays, or 56n2 bytes. In contrast, a banded Cholesky
factor and a banded selected-inverse buffer together require approximately 16n(d + 1)
bytes, excluding ancillary data structures. Figure 8 illustrates these storage relations
for d = 4.
For example, the spectral admission allowance is 640 MiB at n = 4096. At n = 8192
it rises to 2.5 GiB, exceeding the 2 GiB dense allocation limit imposed in the study.
The latter sample length was not evaluated in the present comparison. The curves
therefore represent implementation-specific memory allowances and illustrative buffer
sizes, not measurements of peak resident memory or empirical allocation failures.
There were 970 resource-budget exclusions in the recorded timing outcomes: 776
for the explicit-inverse reference and 194 for SVD. These were induced by prescribed
computational-work thresholds, not observed out-of-memory errors. Thus the memory analysis establishes a concrete feasibility trade-off without attributing numerical
failures to unsupported memory measurements.

5 Discussion
The empirical evidence supports a clear implementation preference, subject to mathematical and computational feasibility: reuse of a spectral decomposition is the
recommended default for finite-difference PLS smoothing-parameter selection over
a moderate-to-large candidate grid. In contrast to a sequence of penalized normalequation factorizations, spectral preprocessing represents the complete search in one
orthogonal basis. It amortizes setup, supplies degrees of freedom and residual sums of
squares directly, and provides a common representation for multiple selection criteria. The advantage is pronounced in the matched CV/GCV grid comparisons and is
compatible with the observed numerical-completion differences.
The recommendation should not be extended beyond the observed evidence. First,
the banded method is often preferable when evaluating only one penalty. Second, the
spectral basis and its work arrays require quadratic memory, which can dominate the
decision for long series even when SVD is attractive at moderate n. Third, numerical
acceptance is not interchangeable with certified forward accuracy: the two implementations apply different diagnostic checks, and both encounter smoothness-inversion
difficulties in the larger fourth-order cases. Fourth, the recorded multi-process execution phases limit the transportability of wall-clock ratios to idle workstations or
different parallel workloads.
These limitations suggest the following practical selection principle. If the task is
a multi-candidate complete-criterion search and the dense spectral workspace satisfies a conservative memory budget, prepare the spectral decomposition once, reuse
series-specific projections and precompute the candidate penalties when appropriate.

If only one candidate is needed, or if dense allocation would be prohibitive, a structured banded method is a reasonable alternative subject to numerical residual checks.
When a requested smoothness value cannot be inverted accurately, neither method
should silently substitute an approximate grid winner: the affected comparison must
be marked invalid or handled by a separately validated numerical strategy.
A further limitation is the restriction to synthetic data and one computational
environment. Seed-cluster intervals quantify variability under the observed experimental protocol, but not variability across operating systems, processors, library versions
or CPU utilization. The validity masks can induce survivor bias in timing ratios
conditional on completed searches. The study does not establish improvements in
trend-estimation risk or forecasting accuracy, and it does not constitute an independent high-precision verification of every evaluated penalty. Future work should isolate
the source of the large-order smoothness-inversion failures, independently certify residual and criterion accuracy, and compare implementations under controlled processor
occupancy.

6 Conclusions
This study establishes that the preferred computational method for penalized leastsquares trend smoothing depends on the parameter-selection workload, not merely the
cost of one linear solve. Across valid matched 100-candidate CV and GCV searches,
14

spectral SVD consistently outperformed banded Cholesky in the representative tested
configurations. A measured conditional-CV setting changed from favoring banded
Cholesky for one candidate to favoring spectral SVD by a factor of approximately 4.85
for the complete grid. The numerical-status analysis further identified repeated banded
post-solve failures on several complete grids, while retaining documented failure modes
of the spectral pipeline.
We therefore recommend spectral decomposition as the primary implementation for
complete, repeated smoothing-parameter selection whenever its dense storage is affordable. Banded methods remain valuable for isolated evaluations and long series, and
numerical diagnostics remain mandatory for either implementation. The contribution
is a measured, criterion-aware recommendation for computational practice rather than
a new trend estimator or an unconditional claim of spectral superiority.
