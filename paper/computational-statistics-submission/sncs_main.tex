\title[Efficient penalized trend computation]{Computational trade-offs in penalized least-squares trend estimation: normalized smoothness, numerical inversion, and solver reuse}
\author*[1]{\fnm{Heriberto} \sur{Espino-Montelongo}}
\author[1]{\fnm{Daniela} \sur{Cort\'es-Toto}}
\affil*[1]{\orgname{Universidad de las Am\'ericas Puebla},
 \orgaddress{\city{San Andr\'es Cholula},\state{Puebla},\country{Mexico}}}

\abstract{Penalized least-squares (PLS) trend smoothing has well-established statistical foundations, but the relative cost of selecting a smoothing parameter depends on the linear-algebra backend, reusability, and parameterization. We present a reproducible comparison organized around the normalized trace smoothness coordinate $S\in[0,1]$, with arbitrary finite-difference order and a fixed or data-derived reference difference mean. The computational study distinguishes complete parameter searches from individual trend fits and $S$-inversion calls. It combines a previously archived 80,640-trial comparison, a paired solver experiment with seed-level bootstrap intervals and multiplicity-adjusted tests, and a float64 comparison of Brent, safeguarded Newton, safeguarded secant, and bisection. End-to-end rankings vary with workload and preparation reuse; inversion performance additionally depends on distance to the smoothness boundaries. Frozen monthly atmospheric CO$_2$ and federal funds observations illustrate CV, GCV, AICc, BIC, conditional Gaussian profile likelihood, and prescribed smoothness for four difference orders. All experiments and figures can be regenerated independently from archived measurements. The results support workload-specific numerical choices rather than a universally best solver or forecasting rule.}
\keywords{penalized least squares, Whittaker--Henderson smoother, numerical algorithms, smoothness inversion, reproducible benchmarking, time series}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:sncs-intro}
Trend smoothing is useful when a noisy observed sequence contains
persistent behavior that must be isolated from short-term variation.
The finite-difference penalized least-squares (PLS) model goes back to
Whittaker and subsequent developments, including Guerrero's formulation
with reference differences \cite{whittaker1923graduation,guerrero2007penalized}.
Selecting the penalty by CV, GCV, AICc, or BIC is well studied
\cite{cortes2017smoothness}, as are structured algorithms for
Whittaker--Henderson problems \cite{weinert2007wh}.
Our aim is \emph{not} to claim the penalty or its spectral representation
as new. Instead, we quantify the engineering trade-offs that occur
when penalties must be repeatedly evaluated, smoothness must be
specified on a common scale, and numerical results must be checked.

The experimental presentation follows the distinction between
\emph{runtime and quality} used in comparisons of inference algorithms,
including Berns et al.\ \cite{berns2022automated}.
Gauss et al.\ \cite{gauss2021skeleton} provide a complementary example
of evaluating smoothing choices against competing performance
requirements, and Cardoso et al.\ \cite{cardoso2025novelty} exemplify
an explicit algorithmic workflow and comparisons against alternatives.
Those studies address different models; their numerical conclusions
are \emph{not} transferred to PLS.

Our contributions are: (i) a common trace-normalized smoothness
coordinate and an explicit inversion-comparison protocol;
(ii) matched-workload measurements of dense, spectral and banded
backends, including paired uncertainty for full-curve evaluation;
and (iii) a reproducible software and graphical study that
preserves numerical outputs independently of figure rendering.
Forecast extensions are illustrative, not a separate forecasting
algorithm.

\section{Penalized trends and computational coordinates}\label{sec:sncs-method}
For equally spaced $y\in\R^n$, let $D_d\in\R^{(n-d)\times n}$
denote the $d$th forward-difference operator, with $1\le d<n$.
For a given $\lambda\ge0$ and reference mean $\mu\in\R$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sncs-pls}
\widehat\tau_{\lambda,\mu}
=\arg\min_{\tau\in\R^n}
\left\{\|y-\tau\|_2^2+
 \lambda\|D_d\tau-\mu\mathbf1\|_2^2\right\}.
\end{equation}
Writing $Q_d=D_d^\top D_d$, the unique finite-penalty solution is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sncs-fit}
\widehat\tau_{\lambda,\mu}=
 (I+\lambda Q_d)^{-1}
 (y+\lambda\mu D_d^\top\mathbf1).
\end{equation}
Since $D_d$ has rank $n-d$, $Q_d$ has $d$ zero eigenvalues.
The null space contains sampled polynomials of degree less than $d$.
For positive eigenvalues $\delta_1,\ldots,\delta_{n-d}$
of $Q_d$, the smoother matrix
$H_\lambda=(I+\lambda Q_d)^{-1}$
has $\tr(H_\lambda)=d+\sum_j(1+\lambda\delta_j)^{-1}$.
Thus the normalized index
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sncs-smoothness}
S(\lambda)=\frac{n-\tr(H_\lambda)}{n-d}
 =\frac1{n-d}\sum_{j=1}^{n-d}
   \frac{\lambda\delta_j}{1+\lambda\delta_j}
\end{equation}
maps $[0,\infty]$ onto $[0,1]$. For finite positive $\lambda$,
\[
 S'(\lambda)=\frac1{n-d}\sum_j
 \frac{\delta_j}{(1+\lambda\delta_j)^2}>0.
\]
Hence each $s\in(0,1)$ has a unique $\lambda=S^{-1}(s)$.
The exact endpoints are $S^{-1}(0)=0$ and $S^{-1}(1)=\infty$.
Evaluating the complementary expression for $1-S$ avoids cancellation
when $s$ approaches one.

For fixed $\mu$, the public implementation supports leave-one-out
CV, GCV, AICc, BIC, conditional Gaussian profile likelihood (MLE),
and controlled $S$. The first five optimize distinct objectives,
while controlled $S$ is a \emph{prescribed target}, not a criterion.
When $\mu=\overline{D_dy}$ is used, the reported CV and MLE
calculations condition on the data-derived reference;
they do not refit $\mu$ inside each deletion or jointly maximize
$(\lambda,\mu)$. Different selectors evaluated on the
\emph{same} finite grid are comparable as selection policies,
but boundary optima do not establish global minimizers.

\section{Numerical experiments}\label{sec:sncs-experiments}
We separate \emph{full curve reconstruction}, \emph{scalar
criterion evaluation}, \emph{repeated parameter selection}, and
\emph{one inverse calculation}. Comparing timings across these
tasks would be misleading: a reused SVD incurs a one-time
factorization but enables many fast spectral evaluations;
a banded Cholesky solve avoids that dense preprocessing for
an isolated fit \cite{weinert2007wh}.

\subsection{End-to-end computation and statistical uncertainty}
The archived author-run Windows factorial experiment comprises
80,640 recorded parameter-search trials, seven backends,
five criteria, $d=0,\ldots,5$, two reference-mean settings,
and distinct reuse scenarios. A second paired study compares
full SVD, symmetric eigendecomposition, and \emph{actual banded
Cholesky} for end-to-end preparation plus full-trend fits.
The latter uses independent simulated-data seeds and
randomized technical timing blocks. One median per independent
seed is the inferential unit. Geometric mean paired time ratios
are obtained from seed-level log ratios, with 95\% \emph{pointwise}
bootstrap intervals and two-sided sign-flip tests adjusted by Holm
over the declared family. Technical repetitions are not
independent observations; performance remains host- and workload-specific.

\subsection{Numerical inversion of normalized smoothness}
Using a prepared spectral smoother, we compare Brent's method
\cite{brent1971zero}, safeguarded Newton, safeguarded secant,
and bisection on 11 target smoothness values from $10^{-14}$
through $1-10^{-14}$, lengths $n=30,60,120$ and orders
$d=0,1,2,3$. Per-target randomized complete blocks record
latency, iterations, function evaluations, convergence failures
and relative \emph{tail} error. The $d=0$ analytic inverse is
excluded from numerical-root speed comparisons. These timings
exclude initial spectral factorization; speed cannot replace
an accuracy or convergence check.

\section{Case study: contemporary monthly data}\label{sec:sncs-data}
Two frozen public series provide contrasting use cases:
441 monthly Mauna Loa CO$_2$ observations (January 1990--September
2026) and 201 US monthly effective federal funds rates
(January 2010--September 2026)
\cite{noaa2026mlo,fred2026fedfunds}.
For $d=1,2,3,4$ and $\mu=0$ or
$\mu=\overline{D_dy_{\rm train}}$, we report selected
$(\lambda,S)$ and a single 12-month historical holdout
for CV, GCV, AICc, BIC, conditional MLE, and controlled
$S=0.86$. The 29 candidate $S$ values used by optimized
selectors are the same within each configuration.
Neither the selected $S$ nor a low in-sample score implies
a better held-out extrapolation.

single-origin errors are diagnostics, not a statistically
validated ranking of future forecasts.

\section{Discussion and conclusion}\label{sec:sncs-conclusion}
The key engineering conclusion is conditional, not absolute:
spectral reuse can be advantageous for repeated
criterion calculations, while specialized banded solvers
can be preferable for other reconstruction workloads.
The measured solver ratios and inversion costs depend on
data length, penalty order, number of evaluations, reuse,
numerical tolerance, and host architecture.
A normalized smoothness coordinate permits interpretable
cross-order comparisons but introduces inversion costs.
Observed selector differences do not, by themselves,
demonstrate an improved forecasting methodology.
The complete 80,640-trial factorial outputs, raw seed-level
paired timings, full inversion targets, and high-resolution
case dashboards are maintained as reproducible supplementary
material rather than repeated in the main article.
