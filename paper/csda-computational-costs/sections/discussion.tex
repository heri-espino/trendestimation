\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
The difference between a mathematical coordinate and a computational
policy is fundamental. The monotone smoothness map $S\leftrightarrow\lambda$
preserves the PLS estimator at corresponding penalty values. It does
not make an arbitrary optimization search faster. Reuse of a translated
candidate grid can reduce repeated work, as illustrated by the
historical matched-grid $n=48$ five-criterion observations:
a 2.668-fold reduction for $d=3$ and 2.823-fold for $d=2$.
These are host-specific examples, not universal speedup guarantees.

The completed Experiment010 measurements extend this distinction to
the choice of \emph{complete numerical backend}. The $n=256,d=1$
GCV results in Table~\ref{tab:experiment010_methods} demonstrate
that a fast structured factorization may lead for one selected
smoothness, whereas the reusable spectral decomposition can lead
for a 100-candidate search. This was also observed across the three
recorded reference-mean policies. The work requested---one fit,
one score, a grid search, or grid reuse over independent series
and criteria---is therefore an essential part of algorithm choice.

\subsection{Why a complete pipeline matters}
The ten named benchmark pipelines are not ten new algebraic
factorizations. The guarded policy selects an existing primitive,
and sparse/iterative hybrids include the cost of an additional
banded selected-inverse diagnostic factorization. A fit-only
PCG or sparse LU solve cannot be compared fairly against
a spectral GCV, CV or marginal-likelihood search with
different outputs. The structure-exploiting
methods of \citet{weinert2007} and recent
Whittaker--Henderson developments by \citet{biessy2026}
are essential prior art. Our table describes the actual
Python implementation, including its additional
diagnostics and memory gates, rather than the
best conceivable implementation of each numerical family.

\subsection{Why numerical validity precedes elapsed time}
High-order differencing can make the finite-penalty systems
very ill-conditioned. The independent 85-digit fitted-trend
audit, the incomplete high-order criterion grids, the
remaining naive-inverse score disagreements, and the
missing $n=512$ spectral-reference comparisons show
why a fast recorded time is not automatically an
admissible solver-selection outcome. In particular,
the MLE correction \eqref{eq:experiment010_mle}
documents a statistical-criterion inconsistency:
omitting a constant from finite-penalty likelihoods
can alter selection once the exact $S=1$
limit is evaluated on a different scale.
The old observations were not erased and the
corrected observations were not backdated.

\subsection{Limits of the completed empirical study}
The completed corrected-source dataset consists of
450 method--case attempts, not all 50,400 planned
method--case entries. The 5570 successful timing rows
include repeated, related workload observations on
one Windows runner and cannot be treated as
independent statistical replications. The two-repeat
$n=256$ comparisons warrant descriptive ranking,
not tight confidence intervals for the prevalence of
a winner across hardware. The measured $n\leq512$
targeted cases do not justify extrapolating a dense
SVD victory to $n=16384$ or $131072$, where
quadratic storage may make it infeasible.
The CHOLMOD optional backend was unavailable, so
its speed cannot be inferred from sparse LU.
The sample-derived reference mean is a conditional
plug-in, not the true latent difference mean and
not jointly estimated ML. The classical CV criterion
is not chronological forecast cross-validation;
this paper does not measure forecast RMSE. The
residual BIC interpolation limit remains statistically
problematic regardless of the numerical backend.

Equation~\eqref{eq:experiment010_selector} gives a
precise decision-theoretic target: minimize predicted
full execution time over the subset of independently
certified, available, memory-feasible pipelines,
with explicit fallback on runtime failure.
However, the present archive does \emph{not}
validate the model constants, holdout crossover
classification, or regret of such a dispatcher.
Those are future experimental questions, not a
hidden conclusion of the present study.

\section{Conclusions}
An interpretable normalized smoothness index can be
used without changing the underlying PLS estimator;
its computational benefit comes from exact mapping
and reuse. The completed ten-policy archive identifies
different measured winners for single-candidate and
full-grid GCV at $n=256,d=1$ under three different
reference-mean policies. It also documents concrete
high-order failures and an analytically corrected
marginal-likelihood normalization inconsistency.
The resulting research contribution is a
\emph{reproducible, numerically gated, evidence-limited
computational comparison}, not a universal fastest
solver or a demonstrated automatic selector.
