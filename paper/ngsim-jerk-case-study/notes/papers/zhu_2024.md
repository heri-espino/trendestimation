# Zhu et al. (2024) — “Two plus one” optimization-based trajectory enhancement

**Citation:** Feng Zhu, Cheng Chang, Zhiheng Li, Boqi Li and Li Li. *Accident Analysis & Prevention* 200:107532 (2024), [DOI 10.1016/j.aap.2024.107532](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aap.2024.107532).

**Inspected:** User-supplied 13-page final Elsevier PDF, including equations, Algorithm 1, CitySim figures/tables and the prediction experiment.

## Problem and high-level method

Existing smoothing can suppress genuinely abrupt acceleration/deceleration and fail to reconcile independently provided positions and velocities. Zhu et al. seek **physical feasibility**, **position–velocity consistency** and **retention of emergencies/maneuvers**.

Their “two plus one” terminology refers to a sequence of two different **norm-based trend filters**, with $\ell_2$ first and $\ell_1$ second. Do not equate their $\ell_2$ stage with our whole procedure.

1. **First stage, $\ell_2$:** Fuse recorded positions and velocities and reduce inconsistency using a quadratic data-fidelity and roughness objective, plus initial-state and kinematic equality constraints.
2. **Second stage, $\ell_1$:** Apply an absolute-difference regularization to acceleration, allowing less aggressive smoothing of some sharp changes; impose acceleration and jerk bounds and the kinematic relations.

In the paper's eqs. (1)–(3), the difference matrix $D$ is a **second-order** difference operator. Their second-stage penalty has the form $\lambda_2\|DA\|_1$ for an acceleration vector $A$: it should **not** be written as $\lambda\|D_3\tau-\mu\mathbf1\|_2^2$. If acceleration itself is obtained by second differences of position, $DA$ is associated with a higher discrete derivative of position, not simply our centered third-difference quadratic.

The paper uses **L-curve** selection of penalty weights (Algorithm 1 and surrounding text, PDF p.5), rather than the normalized smoothness index or the same five selectors as Paper C.

## Physical constraints and evaluation

- Their reported example constraints include **$-8\le a\le5$ m/s²** and **$-15\le j\le15$ m/s³**, motivated by earlier transportation literature (PDF p.4). These are methodological bounds, not universal vehicle or comfort laws.
- **CitySim** is their principal demonstration dataset (pp.6–10), including tests involving hundreds of trajectories (786 in a Freeway-B-01 comparison).
- Their performance assessment includes position/velocity consistency, acceleration and jerk statistics, ability to preserve sharp trajectory changes, and a separate encoder–decoder **LSTM** trajectory-prediction comparison (p.11).
- The downstream prediction evaluation uses average and final displacement errors; it is not the same as our one-step fixed-degree polynomial continuation.

## Strength and limitation relevant to us

**Strength:** The paper shows that high-order regularization for traffic data must consider physical constraints **and preserve actual events**, not simply drive high-frequency derivative content to zero.

**Limitation for direct comparison:** Their model takes more input types and enforces explicit constraints. Our PLS does not repair position–velocity inconsistency when given only positions, and does not impose $a,j$ bounds. Their $\ell_1$ penalty favors piecewise-sparse changes; our squared penalty favors diffuse smoothness and a global reference $\mu$.

**Needed tests:** segment performance by maneuver type, include abrupt changes in synthetic known-truth trajectories, compare our $d=3,\mu=0$ and $\widehat\mu$ against a carefully aligned $\ell_2$–$\ell_1$ reconstruction; report both jerk fidelity and downstream position error.

**Citation placement:** related work and discussion of limitations/physical constraints. Do not claim that quadratic or trend filtering was first introduced to vehicle trajectory data by Paper C.
