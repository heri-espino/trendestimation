\section{Introduction}
Vehicle trajectories provide a physical interpretation for higher-order trend smoothing: the first three time derivatives of longitudinal position are velocity, acceleration and jerk. The US Federal Highway Administration's Next Generation Simulation (NGSIM) dataset reports camera-derived vehicle positions every 0.1 seconds \citep{fhwa2016}. Earlier vehicle-trajectory research emphasizes that estimating acceleration from these observations magnifies measurement noise \citep{thiemann2008}; third derivatives amplify it further. This motivates examining a third-difference penalty ($d=3$), rather than restricting the study to the commonly used second-difference penalty ($d=2$).

We study the PLS model of \citet{guerrero2007}, its trace-based smoothness index \citep{guerrero2008} and classical tuning criteria \citep{cortes2017}. Companion papers examine numerical accuracy and computational complexity. Here we focus on a limited empirical study using recorded vehicle trajectories. We examine the estimated historical trends and assess how a \emph{separate} local extrapolation rule predicts held-out vehicle positions.

The dataset supplies physical position, but not independently measured latent truth or jerk. We do not treat estimated jerk as a direct measurement, add synthetic noise or conflate smoothing, parameter selection, extrapolation and held-out evaluation.

\subsection{The noise amplification motivating third-order smoothing}
If measurement noise $\varepsilon_t$ has independent variance $\sigma_\varepsilon^2$, third forward differentiation uses stencil $(-1,3,-3,1)$, hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:noise}
\operatorname{Var}\!\left(
\frac{D_3\varepsilon_t}{(\Delta t)^3}\right)
=\frac{20\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{(\Delta t)^6}.
\end{equation}
With $\Delta t=0.1$\,s, direct third differences can be extremely unstable. Equation~\eqref{eq:noise} is a conditional mathematical illustration, not an empirical noise-variance estimate: camera-tracking errors may be temporally correlated. Regularization suppresses variation but can bias sudden real changes in jerk. Thus plotted derivatives remain \emph{estimates}, not ground truth.
