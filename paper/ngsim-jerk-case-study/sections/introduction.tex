\section{Introduction}
Vehicle trajectories provide a physical interpretation for higher-order trend smoothing: the first three time derivatives of longitudinal position are velocity, acceleration and jerk. The US Federal Highway Administration's Next Generation Simulation (NGSIM) dataset reports camera-derived vehicle positions every 0.1 seconds \citep{fhwa2016}. Earlier vehicle-trajectory research emphasizes that estimating acceleration from these observations magnifies measurement noise \citep{thiemann2008}; third derivatives amplify it further \citep{ahnert2007,chartrand2011}. This motivates examining a third-difference penalty ($d=3$), rather than restricting the study to the commonly used second-difference penalty ($d=2$).

Independent studies have documented substantial measurement and
reconstruction errors in the NGSIM position records
\citep{punzo2011,coifman2017}, motivating careful separation of
recorded-position forecast error from latent physical truth.
Related reconstruction work has assessed traffic-level consistency
rather than treating raw camera trajectories as error-free
\citep{montanino2015}.

We study the PLS model of \citet{guerrero2007}, its trace-based smoothness index \citep{guerrero2008} and classical tuning criteria \citep{cortes2017}. The present manuscript distinguishes three questions: whether normalized smoothness quantifies relative spectral observation noise under a stated covariance model, whether a third-order fit recovers known physical jerk, and whether a separate continuation rule predicts future camera-recorded positions. Companion papers examine numerical accuracy and computational complexity. We therefore combine rigorously controlled analytic-jerk/noise simulations with chronological comparisons on archived observed vehicle trajectories.

We also compare two boundary continuation rules for the same fitted $d=3$ PLS trend and benchmark them against natural cubic smoothing splines with GCV-selected penalties, Savitzky--Golay differentiation and local polynomial fits. These are standard local/global families for estimating derivatives from noisy experimental data \citep{ahnert2007}, rather than independent numerical matrix-factorization methods. The resulting analysis tests an implementation and application of an established PLS model, not an assertion of historically first use of $d=3$ differences.

\subsection{Prior work and state of the art}
Classical noisy-trajectory reconstruction combines filtering,
interpolation and derivative diagnostics. \citet{punzo2011} assessed
NGSIM accuracy using consistency and jerk-related analyses;
\citet{marczak2012} compared NGSIM I-splines with conventional
filters, including jerk-magnitude and sign-reversal diagnostics.
\citet{montanino2013} explicitly separated removal of unphysical
outliers from suppression of residual high- and medium-frequency
disturbances in a multistage NGSIM reconstruction. Such diagnostics
can flag recording artifacts but cannot by themselves establish
latent physical jerk. Modern video-trajectory approaches include
spatial--temporal embedding and manual-reference validation of
NGSIM trajectories \citep{zhang2024ngsim}, and interpolation
followed by Kalman denoising in separate video-recorded
intersection data \citep{ding2026}; neither supplies an
independent jerk sensor for the NGSIM episodes studied here.

A second family directly incorporates vehicle dynamics or
physical feasibility. The particularly close NGSIM predecessor
of \citet{fan2024} uses \emph{two sequential constrained quadratic
programs}, incorporating position-error envelopes and hard bounds
on speed and higher derivatives, including a third-order
jerk-related choice. These guarantees differ from the
\emph{single unconstrained quadratic penalty} used here.
Other alternatives preserve abrupt motion events through
nonsmooth trend filtering \citep{zhu2024}, exploit separately
measured positions and velocities in adaptive trajectory splines
\citep{cao2021}, or adopt continuous-time stochastic
white-noise-on-jerk priors \citep{tang2019}. More recently,
physics-informed neural reconstruction has addressed sparse
NGSIM cut-in trajectories \citep{xie2026}; its data and objective
are different from position-only jerk recovery.

Finally, \citet{bisoffi2017jerk} estimated \emph{driver-intended}
longitudinal jerk online with an enhanced Kalman observer using
on-vehicle acceleration and pedal-related signals, rather than
offline third differences of camera-recorded position.
The numerical-differentiation literature studies the tradeoff
between noise rejection and derivative fidelity directly
\citep{ahnert2007,chartrand2011,vanbreugel2020}.
Whittaker--Henderson penalization and its extrapolation are
established \citep{whittaker1923,biessy2026}, as are
nonzero-reference PLS and controlled smoothness
\citep{guerrero2007,guerrero2008}.
Our contribution is therefore \emph{not} the invention of
jerk regularization, NGSIM reconstruction, or a new forecasting
model, but a controlled position-only study of reference-jerk
shrinkage, conditional relative-noise calibration, and the
difference between synthetic known-jerk reconstruction and
real recorded-position prediction.

The real NGSIM dataset supplies camera-derived position, but not independently measured latent truth or jerk. We do not contaminate the real positions to create artificial validation labels. Separately, in a fully documented synthetic factorial experiment, we prescribe clean positions and known jerk before adding controlled measurement errors; only those simulated trajectories support direct calibration and true-jerk reconstruction assessments.

\subsection{The noise amplification motivating third-order smoothing}
If measurement noise $\varepsilon_t$ has independent variance $\sigma_\varepsilon^2$, third forward differentiation uses stencil $(-1,3,-3,1)$, hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:noise}
\operatorname{Var}\!\left(
\frac{D_3\varepsilon_t}{(\Delta t)^3}\right)
=\frac{20\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{(\Delta t)^6}.
\end{equation}
With $\Delta t=0.1$\,s, direct third differences can be extremely unstable. Equation~\eqref{eq:noise} is a conditional mathematical illustration, not an empirical noise-variance estimate: camera-tracking errors may be temporally correlated. Regularization suppresses variation but can bias sudden real changes in jerk.
This sensitivity to noise and genuine motion events is documented
in derivative-estimation and trajectory-reconstruction research
\citep{vanbreugel2020,montanino2013,fan2024}.
Thus plotted derivatives remain \emph{estimates}, not ground truth.
