\section{Introduction}
Vehicle trajectories provide a physical interpretation for higher-order trend smoothing: the first three time derivatives of longitudinal position are velocity, acceleration and jerk. The US Federal Highway Administration's Next Generation Simulation (NGSIM) dataset reports camera-derived vehicle positions every 0.1 seconds \citep{fhwa2016}. Earlier vehicle-trajectory research emphasizes that estimating acceleration from these observations magnifies measurement noise \citep{thiemann2008}; third derivatives amplify it further. This motivates examining a third-difference penalty ($d=3$), rather than restricting the study to the commonly used second-difference penalty ($d=2$).

We study the PLS model of \citet{guerrero2007}, its trace-based smoothness index \citep{guerrero2008} and classical tuning criteria \citep{cortes2017}. The present manuscript distinguishes three questions: whether normalized smoothness quantifies relative spectral observation noise under a stated covariance model, whether a third-order fit recovers known physical jerk, and whether a separate continuation rule predicts future camera-recorded positions. Companion papers examine numerical accuracy and computational complexity. We therefore combine rigorously controlled analytic-jerk/noise simulations with chronological comparisons on archived observed vehicle trajectories.

We also compare two boundary continuation rules for the same fitted $d=3$ PLS trend and benchmark them against natural cubic smoothing splines with GCV-selected penalties, Savitzky--Golay differentiation and local polynomial fits. These are standard local/global families for estimating derivatives from noisy experimental data \citep{ahnert2007}, rather than independent numerical matrix-factorization methods. The resulting analysis tests an implementation and application of an established PLS model, not an assertion of historically first use of $d=3$ differences.

This application sits within a substantial prior literature. Whittaker--Henderson penalization predates the present study \citep{whittaker1923}, and extrapolation from penalized smoothers has been developed in modern statistical treatments \citep{biessy2026}. For measured vehicle motion, \citet{marczak2012} examined NGSIM trajectory filters and jerk-related diagnostics, while \citet{cao2021} proposed trajectory splines using position and velocity data. Physically constrained quadratic-programming methods \citep{fan2024} and continuous-time jerk-driven stochastic motion priors \citep{tang2019} address related but distinct problems. Our unconstrained, position-only third-difference PLS is neither a substitute for kinematic feasibility constraints nor a new general forecasting model. The contribution here is a transparent comparison of its reference-jerk and smoothing choices, conditional calibration, and reconstruction versus prediction targets.

The real NGSIM dataset supplies camera-derived position, but not independently measured latent truth or jerk. We do not contaminate the real positions to create artificial validation labels. Separately, in a fully documented synthetic factorial experiment, we prescribe clean positions and known jerk before adding controlled measurement errors; only those simulated trajectories support direct calibration and true-jerk reconstruction assessments.

\subsection{The noise amplification motivating third-order smoothing}
If measurement noise $\varepsilon_t$ has independent variance $\sigma_\varepsilon^2$, third forward differentiation uses stencil $(-1,3,-3,1)$, hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:noise}
\operatorname{Var}\!\left(
\frac{D_3\varepsilon_t}{(\Delta t)^3}\right)
=\frac{20\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{(\Delta t)^6}.
\end{equation}
With $\Delta t=0.1$\,s, direct third differences can be extremely unstable. Equation~\eqref{eq:noise} is a conditional mathematical illustration, not an empirical noise-variance estimate: camera-tracking errors may be temporally correlated. Regularization suppresses variation but can bias sudden real changes in jerk. Thus plotted derivatives remain \emph{estimates}, not ground truth.
