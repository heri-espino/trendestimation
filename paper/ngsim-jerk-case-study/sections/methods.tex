\section{Third-order PLS trend estimation and physical comparisons}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Observation model and third-order difference penalty}
Let $y=(y_1,\ldots,y_n)^\top$ be position measurements in
meters recorded at interval $\Delta t=0.1$\,s and let $\tau$
be their unknown smooth historical trajectory. The $(n-3)\times n$
difference matrix $D_3$ uses the stencil $(-1,3,-3,1)$;
$D_3\tau/(\Delta t)^3$ is a discrete jerk proxy in m/s$^3$.
Following \citet{guerrero2007}, we estimate the trajectory
by minimizing
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pls}
\widehat\tau_{\lambda,\mu}=
\arg\min_{\tau\in\mathbb R^n}
\left\{\|y-\tau\|_2^2+
\lambda\|D_3\tau-\mu\mathbf1\|_2^2\right\},
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ is the reference \emph{discrete} third difference,
and $\lambda\ge0$ controls regularization. Its physically
scaled reference jerk equals $\mu/(\Delta t)^3$.
Writing $Q=D_3^\top D_3$, the unique finite-$\lambda$
solution is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pls-solution}
\widehat\tau_{\lambda,\mu}
=(I+\lambda Q)^{-1}
(y+\lambda\mu D_3^\top\mathbf1).
\end{equation}

The quadratic penalty in Eq.~\eqref{eq:pls} belongs to the established
Whittaker--Henderson family of discrete roughness regularizers
\citep{whittaker1923,weinert2007,biessy2026}. Our nonzero
third-difference reference specializes the prior PLS construction
of \citet{guerrero2007} rather than defining a new smoother.
At $\mu=0$ this is zero-centered squared discrete-jerk
regularization, whereas $\mu\ne0$ shrinks toward
\emph{sustained nonzero discrete jerk}; neither choice
imposes a hard jerk bound. This differs from
the two-stage physically constrained vehicle
reconstruction of \citet{fan2024} and from online
driver-intention jerk filtering with different sensors
\citep{bisoffi2017jerk}.

The $d=3$ polynomial nullspace has dimension three: unpenalized
quadratic position, linear velocity and constant acceleration.
At strong regularization, $\mu=0$ imposes a quadratic limit,
whereas fixed $\mu\ne0$ imposes a cubic position trajectory
with constant discrete third difference. These \emph{limits}
are not assumptions that real vehicles have constant jerk
throughout the observed interval.

\subsection{A bounded smoothness coordinate and its relative-precision meaning}
\label{sec:precision-index}
The conventional \emph{smoothing parameter} $\lambda\ge0$
is unbounded: the bounded quantity is the
\emph{normalized smoothness index} $S(\lambda)$.
Let $\delta_1,\ldots,\delta_m>0$ denote the positive
eigenvalues of $Q=D_3^\top D_3$, with $m=n-3$.
The index used in \texttt{trendestimation} is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:S}
S(\lambda)=\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^{m}
\frac{\lambda\delta_j}{1+\lambda\delta_j}
\in[0,1].
\end{equation}
For finite $\lambda>0$ we have $0<S<1$, while
$S(0)=0$ and $\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}S(\lambda)=1$.
Moreover,
$S'(\lambda)=m^{-1}\sum_j\delta_j
(1+\lambda\delta_j)^{-2}>0$.
A target $s\in(0,1)$ thus specifies a unique
penalty for fixed $n,d$. This is a convenient
dimensionless control scale, but equal numerical
indices need not yield identical trajectories
across different operators or sampling grids.

\paragraph{Historical versus rank-normalized smoothness.}
\citet{guerrero2007} proposed a trace-based
relative-precision index, invoking Theil's
measure, and \citet{cortes2017} clarified
its finite-sample limit. For $d=3$ the original
$n$-normalized index and our $m$-normalized
version satisfy the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:guerrero-normalization}
S_G(\lambda)=1-\frac{\operatorname{tr}(H_\lambda)}n
=\frac{n-3}{n}S(\lambda),\quad
H_\lambda=(I+\lambda Q)^{-1}.
\end{equation}
Three quadratic-nullspace modes remain
unpenalized, so $S_G\to(n-3)/n$, not $1$.
Our definition divides by the $n-3$ positive
penalty directions, giving an exact $[0,1]$
limiting range. Neither this change of
normalization nor the historical underlying
relative-precision idea is a claim of an
entirely new smoothing method.

\paragraph{Meaning of precision.}
Under the independent second-moment model
$y=\tau+\varepsilon$,
$D_3\tau=\mu\mathbf1+u$, with
$\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon)=
\sigma_\varepsilon^2 I$,
$\operatorname{Cov}(u)=\sigma_u^2 I$,
$\operatorname{Cov}(u,\varepsilon)=0$,
and positive variances, we have
$\lambda=\sigma_\varepsilon^2/\sigma_u^2$.
The observational and roughness contributions
to the associated information matrix are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jerk-precision-matrix}
\begin{aligned}
J&=\sigma_\varepsilon^{-2}I+
   \sigma_u^{-2}Q
   =\sigma_\varepsilon^{-2}(I+\lambda Q),\\
P&=\sigma_u^{-2}Q .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
This construction follows the relative-precision
treatment of \citet{guerrero2007,cortes2017}.
The roughness component $P$ has rank $n-3$:
its three unpenalized quadratic-position
directions make the intrinsic prior improper,
but the observation term makes $J$
strictly positive definite.

Under the additional independent \emph{Gaussian}
innovation model, with diffuse unpenalized
polynomial coefficients, $J$ is the
\emph{conditional latent-trend precision}
and the conditional covariance is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jerk-posterior-covariance}
\operatorname{Cov}(\tau\mid y)
=J^{-1}=\sigma_\varepsilon^2H_\lambda,
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ and both variance components
are treated as fixed. This model-based
posterior covariance should not be confused
with the repeated-sampling covariance of a
fitted smoother for an unknown fixed trend.

Consequently, the historical Theil-inspired
relative-precision trace, here normalized
over the $n-3$ penalized directions, is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:relative-precision}
\frac{\operatorname{tr}(PJ^{-1})}{n-3}
=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(I-H_\lambda)}{n-3}
=S(\lambda).
\end{equation}
This is \emph{relative precision attributable
to the smoothness component, averaged over
penalized directions}, not absolute
camera-position sensor precision. Equivalently,
at the true variance ratio, $S$ is the
average spectral fraction of observed
third-difference covariance caused by
differenced measurement error; Section~
\ref{sec:precision} tests that conditional
implication experimentally. The trace
algebra itself requires only second moments:
Gaussianity is needed for the posterior
covariance interpretation and the subsequent
likelihood-based selection.

\paragraph{Conditional uncertainty for discrete jerk.}
At sampling interval $\Delta t$, put
$j_d=D_3\tau/(\Delta t)^3$ in m/s$^3$.
The same Gaussian model implies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jerk-conditional-covariance}
\operatorname{Cov}(j_d\mid y)
=\frac{D_3J^{-1}D_3^\top}{(\Delta t)^6},
\end{equation}
conditional on fixed $\mu$ and both
variance components. This is a theoretical
uncertainty identity, not an empirically
calibrated confidence interval: the
frozen experiments do not test posterior
interval coverage, and real NGSIM episodes
provide no independently observed true jerk.
Data-selected $\lambda$, estimated $\mu$,
correlated camera noise, and model
misspecification require further analysis.

\paragraph{Effective complexity and limits.}
For fixed $\mu$ and fixed $\lambda$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:edf}
\operatorname{df}(\lambda)
=\operatorname{tr}(H_\lambda)
=n-(n-3)S(\lambda).
\end{equation}
Trace-based model complexity is classical
\citep{efron2004,ye1998}. As $S$ goes
from 0 to 1, the conditional effective
degrees of freedom decrease from $n$ to
three. Additional adaptivity from estimating
$\mu$ or choosing $\lambda$ is excluded.
Nor does $S$ determine absolute noise
variance: multiplying both observation
noise and genuine jerk-innovation standard
deviations by the same constant leaves
their ratio (and theoretical $S$) unchanged.

\subsection{Advantages and boundaries of reference-mean third-order PLS}
\label{sec:pls-advantages}
The established PLS framework offers six
useful \emph{design properties} in this
position-only vehicle application.
\emph{First}, its fixed-parameter objective
is a strictly convex quadratic: since
$I+\lambda D_3^\top D_3$ is positive
definite, the trend fit is unique for
every finite $\lambda\ge0$.
\emph{Second}, the chosen difference
order directly regularizes a physically
scaled discrete-jerk proxy, rather than
assuming that a low position-fitting
error ensures accurate derivatives.
\emph{Third}, a nonzero reference $\mu$
permits sustained signed jerk instead of
silently forcing the constant-acceleration
polynomial limit implied by $\mu=0$
\citep{guerrero2007}.
\emph{Fourth}, the filter applies
continuous spectral shrinkage rather
than the discontinuous hard truncation
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsvd-filter}.
\emph{Fifth}, its bounded, monotone
$S$ index gives transparent smoothing
control, an explicit conditional
degrees-of-freedom identity and a
testable model-dependent relative-precision
interpretation \citep{guerrero2007,guerrero2008,cortes2017}.
\emph{Sixth}, the fixed-order difference
penalty is sparse and banded, allowing
structured linear algebra without
forming a dense inverse
\citep{weinert2007,biessy2026}.

These properties do \emph{not}
imply universal accuracy or robustness.
In particular, this unconstrained
quadratic smoother supplies no hard
speed/acceleration/jerk feasibility
bounds \citep{fan2024}, does not
guarantee preservation of abrupt motion,
and does not forecast without a
separate continuation rule.
The distinctive contribution of
the present work is the \emph{combined
physical application and conditional
empirical assessment} of reference
jerk, $[0,1]$ precision-related
smoothness, known-jerk reconstruction
and recorded-future position prediction,
not invention of these components.

\subsection{Smoothing, reference jerk and choice of regularization}
We compare the existing PLS policies: a fixed reference
$\mu=0$; the sample mean of the noisy third differences;
a global cubic polynomial fitted to \emph{historical}
positions (its discrete third difference gives $\mu$);
and a jointly estimated reference from Gaussian restricted
likelihood. For the stochastic interpretation,
$\lambda=\sigma_\varepsilon^2/\sigma_u^2$ is the ratio
of observation noise variance to genuine discrete
third-difference innovation variance. The Gaussian
interpretation adds distributional assumptions beyond
the second-moment PLS construction of \citet{guerrero2007}.
When $\mu$ is estimated rather than fixed, the
mean-adjusted restricted likelihood must also eliminate
its nuisance mean direction. Restricted maximum likelihood is an
established variance-component method
\citep{patterson1971,harville1977}; the fixed-effect
adjustment discussed by \citet{harville1977} motivates
removing the estimated reference direction.
Our particular unknown-reference REML objective is still
derived for the stated third-difference covariance model,
not copied from a classical mixed-effects formula.

We retain the library's GCV and Gaussian profile-ML choices
and explicitly separate \emph{fixed-$\mu$ profile likelihood}
from \emph{joint unknown-$\mu$ REML}; they need not minimize
the same physical loss. Ordinary and generalized cross-validation for smoothing parameter
selection have a substantial classical literature
\citep{craven1979,weinert2007}; the corrected Akaike criterion is
associated with \citet{hurvich1998}. The original historical case
also screened LOOCV, AICc and restricted residual BIC,
as shown in its archived selection comparison. Here
they are background sensitivity analyses, not a paper
about computational methods or root-finding performance.
All tuning uses the training portion, never the held-out future.

\subsection{Truncated SVD as an \emph{estimator} baseline}
A full singular-value decomposition used solely to evaluate
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pls-solution} is a numerical implementation
of \emph{the same PLS estimator}, not a competitor. For a
meaningful comparison we instead use \emph{hard spectral
truncation} of the \emph{same} third-difference operator.
Write
\begin{equation}
D_3=U\,\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_m)V_+^\top,
\qquad 0<\sigma_1\le\cdots\le\sigma_m,
\end{equation}
where the $V_+$ columns are ordered from lowest to
highest difference roughness, and let $V_0$ span
$\ker D_3$. Choose $q$ satisfying
$D_3q=\mu\mathbf1$, for example
$q_i=\mu\binom{i-1}{3}$. PLS may be written as a
\emph{soft spectral filter}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pls-filter}
\widehat\tau_{\rm PLS}
=q+V_0V_0^\top(y-q)
+\sum_{j=1}^{m}
\frac{v_jv_j^\top(y-q)}{1+\lambda\sigma_j^2}.
\end{equation}
\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
The truncated-SVD comparison is instead the
\emph{hard cutoff}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tsvd-filter}
\widehat\tau_{{\rm TSVD},r}
=q+V_0V_0^\top(y-q)+
\sum_{j=1}^{r}v_jv_j^\top(y-q),
\quad 0\le r\le m.
\end{equation}
This is a genuine, distinct low-roughness spectral
smoother. We specify two training-only comparisons:
(i) choose $r$ by conditional GCV over ranks
$0,\ldots,m-1$; (ii) nominally match the conditional
PLS complexity with
$r=\operatorname{round}(\operatorname{df}_{\rm PLS}-3)$.
The latter is a matched-complexity \emph{ablation}, not
an independently optimized SVD procedure. Both use the
same training-only global cubic reference $\mu$ as
the corresponding profile-ML PLS comparator.
Their normalized hard-filter index is
$S_{\mathrm{TSVD}}=1-r/m$.

\paragraph{Conditioning on the estimated reference.}
With $P_r$ the projector onto the $3+r$ retained
modes, the implemented TSVD rank criterion is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tsvd-conditional-gcv}
\mathrm{GCV}_{\rm conditional}(r)
=\frac{n\| (I-P_r)(y-q_{\widehat\mu})\|^2}
{[n-(3+r)]^2},\qquad 0\le r<m.
\end{equation}
It uses the training-estimated cubic $\widehat\mu$ in
the residual but treats that reference as fixed
in the denominator $n-(3+r)=m-r$. It is therefore
\emph{conditional plug-in GCV}, not exact GCV
for the entire data-dependent fitting map.
In particular, retaining $r$ near $m$ leaves
few nominal residual degrees of freedom.

The same issue makes effective-complexity matching
\emph{nominal} rather than exact. Write
$\widehat\mu=a^\top y$, where $a$ is the linear
cubic-OLS reference functional, $q_\mu=\mu q_1$
with $D_3q_1=\mathbf1$, and let $P_r$ project
onto the $3+r$ retained TSVD directions.
For fixed $\lambda$ and $r$ (ignoring their
additional data-driven selection), the
linear fits have effective degrees of freedom
\begin{align}
\operatorname{df}_{\rm PLS}^{\rm plug}
 &=\operatorname{tr}(H_\lambda)
  +a^\top(I-H_\lambda)q_1,\\
\operatorname{df}_{\rm TSVD}^{\rm plug}
 &=3+r+a^\top(I-P_r)q_1 .
\end{align}
These expressions follow from the linear maps at fixed tuning;
they do not include the extra adaptivity caused by selecting a
penalty or cutoff from the observations
\citep{ye1998,efron2004}.
A fixed-rank GCV denominator for the complete
plug-in linear map would instead be
$n-\operatorname{df}_{\rm TSVD}^{\rm plug}$.
The rank matching in Experiment008 equates
only the fixed-reference trace components;
it does not equate these full plug-in
degrees of freedom. Both reference
estimates are based on historical data only.
Hard spectral cutoff and continuous Tikhonov-type attenuation
are classical regularization alternatives \citep{hansen1990tsvd}.
The analysis of ill-posed inverse problems explains why
retaining poorly determined singular modes can amplify errors;
it does not supply a universal accuracy ranking between
the present TSVD and PLS jerk estimators.
The use of the \emph{roughness} operator's right singular vectors
here is our specified estimator comparison, not a claim that this
particular position-fitting rank rule was validated by Hansen.
Additional inverse-problem regularization treatments include
\citet{renaut2019tsvd}.

\subsection{Other physical jerk and trajectory baselines}
The baseline families are (i) natural cubic smoothing
splines with their second-derivative roughness penalty
chosen by training-only GCV, (ii) Savitzky--Golay local
polynomial smoothing (window 21, degree three),
(iii) ordinary local polynomial and constant-velocity
or constant-acceleration extrapolations, and
(iv) raw third differences as an intentionally noisy
derivative reference \citep{reinsch1967,wahba1990,craven1979,savitzky1964,ahnert2007,cao2021}.
A modern comparative treatment emphasizes that derivative
estimator parameters should be selected with respect to noisy
differentiation objectives \citep{vanbreugel2020}; its
methods were \emph{not} additional implemented baselines
in the frozen Experiment008.
A classical cubic spline penalizes the \emph{second}
derivative, whereas Eq.~\eqref{eq:pls} penalizes
\emph{deviations of the third difference from $\mu$};
these are different physical smoothness assumptions.
The comparison measures historical discrete jerk only
when a synthetic trajectory supplies known truth, and
future position when a chronological future is available.

\subsection{Forecast continuation is separate from historical smoothing}
Given a fitted historical trend, the primary $d=3$
continuation assumes constant future third difference
equal to the chosen reference $\mu$. At future
integer horizon $h$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:d3-recurrence}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat\tau_N(h)
&=\widehat\tau_N+h\nabla\widehat\tau_N\\
&\quad+\binom{h+1}{2}\nabla^2\widehat\tau_N
+\binom{h+2}{3}\mu.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
This is the algebraic $d=3$ extension of the
lower-order forecasting construction of
\citet{guerrero2007}, \emph{not a new
probabilistic prediction model}. A secondary
continuation fits a quadratic to the last 25
fitted positions after subtracting the polynomial
particular solution $q$, then adds the future $q$.
Both schemes use only historical data; neither can
anticipate future changes in driver behavior.
The chronological separation between model fitting and future
evaluation follows the general logic of out-of-sample forecast
assessment \citep{tashman2000,cerqueira2020}; it is not equivalent
to randomized pointwise cross-validation of a dependent trajectory.
A separate spline baseline uses a quadratic Taylor
continuation from spline endpoint derivatives.
The pooled forecast error at horizon $H\in\{5,10,20\}$
is a \emph{cumulative evaluation-window RMSE}, not
an error at only the final future frame:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:forecast-window-rmse}
\mathrm{RMSE}_{H} = \left\{\frac{1}{NH}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{h=1}^{H}
\bigl[\widehat x_i(h)-x_i(h)\bigr]^2\right\}^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
Here $N=1{,}296$ for synthetic confirmation and
$N=200$ for NGSIM, and $H$ counts frames at
$\Delta t=0.1$\,s. Thus ``1-s RMSE'' is the
root mean square over frames $1,\ldots,10$,
\emph{not} just the position at $t=1$\,s.
The $0.5$, $1$, and $2$-s windows overlap.
The recorded NGSIM position remains an
observed proxy, not latent ground truth.

\subsection{Two complementary experimental cases}
\paragraph{Known-truth simulation.}
In the completed Experiment005, we generated $1{,}296$
confirmatory analytic trajectories spanning six jerk
regimes, three observation-noise levels and three error
structures, with 80 historical observations and 20
clean future observations at 10\,Hz. Historical jerk
is scored against the \emph{same discrete operator}
applied to the true clean positions, not against an
unmatched instantaneous derivative. Experiment006
compared fixed and estimated references and Gaussian
parameter selection on these preserved cases. The
new Experiment008 compares the PLS and TSVD filters
with splines and Savitzky--Golay on \emph{exactly
these archived simulations}, without regenerating
them or refitting the original PLS parameter search.
Experiment007 separately generated $1{,}944$ paired
noise--dynamics calibration conditions, based on
72 independent innovation parents; its known-covariance
analysis is described in Section~\ref{sec:precision}.

\paragraph{Real recorded trajectories.}
The Federal Highway Administration/USDOT NGSIM
dataset (DOI: 10.21949/1504477) supplies camera-derived
longitudinal vehicle position at 10\,Hz
\citep{fhwa2016}. Source \texttt{Local\_Y} values
are converted from feet to meters by $0.3048$.
The initial descriptive illustration used 15 overlapping
chronological windows from five US-101 track segments
that contain only two distinct integer vehicle IDs.
A larger fixed-source follow-up selected 200 quality-qualified
episodes (100 US-101 and 100 I-80); their IDs
cannot be equated with 200 unique physical vehicles.
Every episode supplies 80 historical positions and
withholds $h=5,10,20$ future frames at a prespecified
origin. The new TSVD comparison reuses the
\emph{same} archived 200 episodes and training-only
PLS parameter choices. Real jerk truth and sensor
precision are \emph{not independently observed}.
The 200 episodes represent five coarse recording-time
blocks, so pooled forecasting scores describe this
convenience sample, not independent vehicle-population
inference. Source queries, file checksums, configuration
and raw/fitted outcome rows remain documented separately
for every experiment.
