\section{Discussion and conclusions}
The vehicle-position data show why difference order, reference mean and extrapolation rule must be distinguished. For a position series, $d=3$ has a direct relation to the third temporal derivative---jerk---but neither the raw third difference nor its smoothed counterpart is an independent physical reference signal. A smooth estimated jerk curve may miss abrupt maneuvers.

The normalized coordinate $S\in[0,1]$ represents the fraction of penalizable effective degrees of freedom removed. Under the independent Gaussian variance-ratio model, it also equals the average spectral fraction of third-difference variance due to observation noise. Experiment007 validates this conditional interpretation against independently known simulated truth, while showing that correlated measurement errors can cause considerable calibration bias. Crucially, $S$ alone does not identify the absolute observation-noise variance: scaling both noise and genuine jerk volatility together preserves the relative index. Its definition is consistent across difference orders and its inverse is independent of a \emph{fixed} reference-difference mean; fitted degrees of freedom change when that mean is itself estimated.

The favorable one-second held-out result for GCV-selected $d=3$ is descriptive. This small sample does not establish a general forecasting advantage for vehicle trajectories. The fifteen training/holdout origins overlap within five recorded segments and do not justify naive independent-window inference. Selection criteria may yield nearly identical smooth trends, and the observed position itself includes unmeasured camera noise.

The training-data mean-difference policy uses no future information, but CV scores computed conditionally on that plug-in mean are not exact re-estimated LOOCV. Profiled Gaussian marginal likelihood requires variance assumptions; it is not point-estimator MLE for $\tau$ or joint ML estimation of $\mu$. Restricted residual BIC remains an exploratory constrained criterion because the original unrestricted interpolation boundary is mathematically pathological.

The joint Gaussian REML extension illustrates a further distinction: statistically well-calibrated relative noise attribution does not imply minimal historical jerk-reconstruction or forecast error. Experiment006 found lower known-jerk RMSE for the global cubic OLS reference with continuous profiled likelihood, and its larger real NGSIM comparison continued to favor a GCV-selected smoothing spline in pooled recorded-future position error. On 161 of 200 real NGSIM episodes, continuous REML selected the zero-smoothing boundary; this emphasizes model sensitivity rather than genuine noise-free observations.\n\nThe matched recurrence and GCV-spline comparisons sharpen this limitation. At the shortest horizon, the $d=3$ PLS recurrence using $\mu=0$ yields the lowest observed-future position RMSE among the reported methods; at two seconds the GCV-selected spline with a quadratic continuation performs better. Moreover, the training-derived nonzero reference mean helps the 25-point continuation in the present pilot but not the three-point recurrence. Thus an apparent benefit of $\mu\ne0$ cannot be attributed solely to smoothing without separating the historical estimator from the extrapolator. The five track segments correspond to only \emph{two distinct vehicle identities}; replication on independent vehicles is essential before generalizing these numerical rankings.

Forecasts come from a local polynomial fitted to the historical PLS trend. It is \emph{not} a predictive distribution, and it cannot anticipate unobserved changes in acceleration, traffic interactions or lane changes. Future research requires independent maneuvers, substantially more vehicles, genuine measurement-error characterization, and a dedicated dynamic/state-space forecast comparison. The seven fitting backends are benchmarked for single-curve cost and numerical agreement; complete-selection runtime and independent multiprecision accuracy are addressed by companion studies.
