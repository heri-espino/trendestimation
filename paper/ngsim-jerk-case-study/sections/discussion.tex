\section{Discussion and conclusions}
The main question of this work is how to extract a physically
interpretable third-difference trend from measured position,
not how quickly competing matrix factorizations solve
the \emph{same} penalized least-squares equations.
The established PLS estimator provides a direct penalty
on deviations of discrete jerk from a reference mean and
an intuitive normalized smoothing coordinate $S$.

Under the independent Gaussian variance-ratio model,
$S$ equals the mean spectral fraction of observed
third-difference covariance attributable to observation
error. The controlled paired calibration experiment
supports this interpretation quantitatively when the
covariance assumptions hold. Its stress tests also
identify a clear failure: serially correlated measurement
noise can bias $S$ substantially even when its rank
association with relative noise persists. No value
of $S$ alone can identify absolute observation-noise
precision, since scaling both genuine jerk volatility
and measurement error by the same amount preserves
their variance ratio.

A second known-truth simulation case provides a
different outcome: PLS with the historical cubic-OLS
reference and continuous Gaussian profile likelihood
has lower discrete-jerk RMSE than a hard spectral
SVD cutoff at nominally matched fixed-reference degrees
of freedom, and much lower RMSE than the position-GCV
truncation in the tested regimes. Hard cutoff and
continuous shrinkage are \emph{different estimators};
using a full SVD in place of a Cholesky factorization
of the PLS normal equations would not give such
a statistical comparison. The surprising sensitivity
of GCV-truncated SVD to third differentiation illustrates
that selecting a smooth position path and recovering
higher-order derivatives need not be equivalent goals.
This result is conditional on the tested spectral
basis, reference mean policies and noise-generating
processes; it is not a universal rejection of
SVD-based denoising, smoothing splines, or local filters.

The real recorded-position results are more cautious.
Across 200 quality-qualified NGSIM episodes,
the three-point PLS and nominally fixed-reference-
complexity-matched SVD forecasts are similar; a GCV natural cubic
smoothing spline with quadratic Taylor continuation
has the lowest pooled recorded-future position window RMSE
among the reported procedures. The smaller original
US-101 exploratory sample favored the PLS
recurrence at 0.5\,s; its five segments came
from only two integer vehicle identifiers and
cannot establish a general forecasting advantage.
The larger sample contains multiple shared
recording-time blocks, not 200 demonstrably
independent physical drivers. True jerk and
latent clean position are not measured in NGSIM.
Consequently forecast accuracy against camera
positions is not evidence of physical jerk accuracy.

The reference third-difference mean $\mu$ also
matters: a nonzero global cubic OLS reference
can improve simulated jerk reconstruction without
making joint Gaussian REML or a particular
future extrapolator universally optimal. The
last-three-point recurrence assumes constant
third difference \emph{after the boundary}, and
does not provide a predictive distribution
or anticipate unobserved driver actions.
These differences explain why smoothness
calibration, jerk recovery and future-position
forecasting should be assessed as
\emph{separate} estimands.

Our contribution is therefore a reproducible
physical application and conditional
characterization of third-order PLS and its
interpretable smoothness index. We do not
claim to have invented PLS, third-order penalties,
the smoothness index, or SVD truncation.
Future work should use additional independent
recording scenes, credible external jerk/position
ground truth, experimentally characterized
sensor-error covariance, and competing
trajectory/state-space methods. Numerical
backend timing, optimized factorization
strategies and many-parameter selection tensors
belong in separate computational-statistics
manuscripts rather than this physical case study.

\section*{Reproducibility and data rights}
The US DOT/FHWA NGSIM dataset \citep{fhwa2016} is credited as the sole source of observed positions. Its official provider portal identifies a CC BY-SA 3.0 license; the dataset identifier and DOI are given in the cited reference. Source query and extraction rules, tracked vehicle identifiers, segment starting timestamps, immutable checksums, chronological forecast rows, solver diagnostics, and original TeX-backed PGF figure sources are preserved under the case-study directory. The Experiment005--008 studies and their follow-up analyses are archived separately. These archives preserve generated synthetic ground truth and observation errors, historical parameter selections, the frozen 1,296-trajectory PLS--TSVD--spline--Savitzky--Golay comparisons, 72-parent noise-calibration data, paired uncertainty analyses, source-based figures, environment records, exact file paths and SHA-256 inventories. No independently measured jerk ground truth is available for the real NGSIM cases. The original official-position subset has not been artificially contaminated or pre-smoothed. Figure-derived jerk is explicitly estimated rather than observed.
