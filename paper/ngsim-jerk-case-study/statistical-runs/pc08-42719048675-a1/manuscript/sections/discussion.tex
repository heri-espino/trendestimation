\section{Discussion and conclusions}
\label{sec:discussion}
This study examines a \emph{statistical smoothing
methodology} and its physical interpretation,
rather than an algorithmic speed comparison.
Third-order penalized least squares (PLS)
places an explicit penalty on deviations
of discrete jerk from a chosen reference.
The normalized smoothing coordinate
$S\in[0,1]$ has an exact relationship
to conditional effective degrees of
freedom and allows regularization
strength to be described without relying
on the arbitrary magnitude of $\lambda$.
This remains useful even where PLS is
not the smallest-RMSE method.

\subsection{Statistical meaning and identifiability}
Under the independent Gaussian
variance-ratio model,
$S$ is the average spectral fraction
of the \emph{third-difference covariance}
attributable to observation error,
not the proportion of erroneous position
observations. The controlled factorial
simulation confirms that the
joint-REML-selected index can be
well calibrated to this theoretical
fraction when the assumptions hold:
Spearman association $0.965$ and mean
absolute calibration error $0.0115$
in the independently Gaussian condition.

We also varied two quantities that
should not be conflated. Increasing
measurement noise with true jerk
volatility fixed raises $S$;
increasing genuine jerk innovation
volatility with measurement noise
fixed lowers it. Increasing both
scales proportionally leaves $S$
unchanged, even when absolute
observation-noise variance changes
sixteenfold. Consequently, the index
is a model-conditional \emph{relative
noise-attribution measure}, not a
universal indicator of camera
accuracy or absolute measurement
precision. Serially correlated
observation errors induce substantial
calibration bias; these failures
must accompany the favorable
model-correct results.

\subsection{Recovery of jerk is distinct from forecasting}
A second simulation family contains
known analytic motion and its true
discrete jerk. In that family the
global cubic OLS reference with
profiled Gaussian likelihood
performed well for historical jerk
recovery. Yet the preferred
reference policy changes when
evaluating future \emph{clean
position}: fixed $\mu=0$ with
continuous profile likelihood
had lower one-second forecast
RMSE among the tested PLS variants.
Properly mean-adjusted joint REML
optimizes a likelihood, not
the jerk-reconstruction or
forecasting risk, and was not
uniformly best under either loss.

These differences do not undermine
the interpretability of $S$.
They demonstrate that smoothness
selection, estimation of the
reference third difference,
and boundary continuation are
distinct components of the
statistical model. A local
25-point polynomial continuation
and a last-three-point recurrence
can forecast very differently
from the same fitted trend.
Neither is a complete probabilistic
forecast model for a driver whose
acceleration changes unexpectedly.

\subsection{What the recorded NGSIM comparison establishes}
The 200-episode real-data application
is an important counterweight
to favorable synthetic outcomes.
For the recorded-future position
criterion, a GCV-selected natural
cubic smoothing spline with
quadratic Taylor continuation
has the lowest pooled RMSE
at all examined horizons.
The PLS three-point recurrence
is relatively close at 0.5\,s,
but its longer-horizon error
is larger. Joint REML selects the
zero-smoothing boundary in 161
of 200 real episodes, highlighting
a sensitivity to Gaussian noise
assumptions and boundary selection,
not an assertion that the camera
measurements are noise free.

A separate exploratory five-segment
pilot occasionally favors a
particular PLS extrapolator, but
its 15 origins overlap and
represent only two reported
vehicle IDs. It cannot outweigh
the broader comparison.
Moreover, the larger 200-episode
sample comes from only five
coarse recording-time blocks,
and repeated reported IDs need
not denote the same physical
driver. Neither convenience
sample supports robust
population-level claims of
statistical superiority. The
real data contain no independently
observed true jerk and thus
cannot validate physical
jerk reconstruction directly.

\subsection{Scope of the contribution}
The underlying PLS estimator,
difference penalties, and
percentage smoothness have
important historical precedents
in the work of
\citet{guerrero2007,guerrero2008}
and subsequent comparisons by
\citet{cortes2017}. We do not
claim to have invented PLS
smoothing or general PLS
forecasting. The applied
contribution here is to
combine a third-difference
kinematic interpretation,
an explicit normalized
smoothness coordinate,
reference-jerk policies,
known-truth calibration
and recovery experiments,
and a real NGSIM comparison
against established
smoothing methods.

Further investigation should
use independently documented
measurement noise or multiple
sensors, more independent
recording sessions, and
covariance models allowing
serial correlation. A
dynamic physical forecasting
model is also necessary
before making broad claims
about future maneuver prediction.

\section*{Reproducibility and data rights}
The U.S. DOT/FHWA NGSIM dataset
\citep{fhwa2016} supplies
the observed positions; the
dataset portal identifies
a CC BY-SA 3.0 license.
Source queries and original
page hashes, episode catalogs,
all forecasts and model-selection
rows, and the distinction
between recorded and simulated
truth are documented in
the case-study repository.
The two simulation studies
preserve every generated
clean position, noisy
observation, true discrete
jerk, reference-mean policy,
fit error and selected $S$.
All source code, simulation
configurations, first-party
data, bootstrap summaries,
vector figures, and
run-specific SHA-256
inventories are retained
in the Experiment004--007
and manuscript artifact
archives. No underlying
NGSIM observation was
artificially altered to
create purported real
ground-truth jerk.
