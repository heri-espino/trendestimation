\begin{abstract}
We describe \texttt{trendestimation}, reproducible scientific software for finite-difference penalized least-squares trends with admissible difference orders and nonzero reference differences. We derive the $d$-dimensional nullspace, diagonal attenuation factors, and a corrected trace-based smoothness index spanning $[0,1]$. We explain the speed and limitations of a reusable singular-value decomposition; no GPU execution claim is made. We also investigate Gaussian marginal maximum likelihood and obtain a closed-form reference-difference mean estimator conditional on the smoothing parameter, distinct from the released plug-in procedure. Reproducible simulations and historical atmospheric carbon-dioxide measurements compare difference orders and illustrate when automatic selection may favor near-interpolating fits. The contribution is mathematical synthesis, tested computational integration, and a reproducibility workflow, not invention of PLS or marginal likelihood.
\end{abstract}
\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} penalized least squares; trend estimation; finite differences; spectral decomposition; likelihood profiling; smoothness correction; scientific Python.

\section{Introduction}
An observed time series may combine low-frequency behavior with temporary movements and measurement noise. Estimating its trend is a descriptive task: the resulting curve depends on how fidelity to the observations is traded against smoothness. Whittaker's graduation framework and subsequent finite-difference penalized least-squares (PLS) estimators formalize this compromise \cite{whittaker1923graduation,guerrero2007penalized}. The Hodrick--Prescott filter is an important second-difference special case, but the statistical problem admits other penalty orders and nonzero reference values for the differences \cite{cortes2017smoothness}.

The tuning parameter must also be chosen. Cort\'es-Toto, Guerrero, and Reyes \cite{cortes2017smoothness} investigate attained smoothness when choosing the penalty with cross-validation (CV), generalized cross-validation (GCV), corrected Akaike information criterion (AICc), or Bayesian information criterion (BIC). Their simulations vary trend shape, series length, variability, and seasonality. Efficient Whittaker--Henderson computation has an extensive history, including methods exploiting banded factorization and structure to reduce numerical expense \cite{weinert2007wh}; consequently, diagonalization is not presented here as a newly discovered mathematical algorithm.

Within the \emph{Bolet\'in de la Sociedad Mexicana de Computaci\'on Cient\'ifica y sus Aplicaciones}, Balam et al. \cite{balam2025overview} compare normal equations, QR, and singular-value decomposition (SVD) in least-squares computation and discuss conditioning. Azofeifa and Moreles \cite{azofeifa2025hybrid} present a different numerical model, but similarly distinguish the formulation, solver, numerical comparisons, and limitations. We follow this expository pattern while studying a separate statistical problem.

Our objective is to provide a small, auditable Python package, \texttt{trendestimation}, that implements the \emph{published} PLS family and exposes examples as executable notebooks within Sphinx documentation. Specifically, we describe the affine solution for general order $d$ and reference mean $\mu$, its spectral implementation, the criteria available in the public API, and a reproducible numerical illustration. Experimental forecast-horizon cross-validation and dynamic tracking of minima from other research projects are not included. The distinction between an in-sample smoother and an ex ante forecast is maintained throughout.

\section{Model and spectral representation}
\subsection{Finite-difference penalized least squares}
Let $y=(y_1,\ldots,y_n)^\top\in\R^n$ denote an equally spaced series. For an integer $1\le d<n$, let $D_d\in\R^{(n-d)\times n}$ represent the $d$th forward-difference operator; for example, row entries of $D_2$ are $(1,-2,1)$. We consider the published estimator
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
 \widehat\tau_{\lambda,\mu}=\arg\min_{\tau\in\R^n}\left\{\|y-\tau\|_2^2+\lambda\|D_d\tau-\mu\mathbf{1}\|_2^2\right\},\qquad\lambda\ge0,
\end{equation}
where $\mu\in\R$ is a fixed reference mean for the differences. The choice $\mu=0$ gives the familiar pure penalty. The implementation additionally permits $d=0$, with $D_0=I_n$, as a well-defined edge case; the geometric statements below concern $1\le d<n$.

Write $Q_d=D_d^\top D_d$ and choose $p\in\R^n$ satisfying $D_dp=\mu\mathbf1$. Using zero-based indices $t=0,\ldots,n-1$, one convenient choice is $p_t=\mu\binom{t}{d}$ because $D_d\binom{t}{d}=1$. Setting $x=\tau-p$ and differentiating the strictly convex objective gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:affine}
 \widehat\tau_{\lambda,\mu}=p+H_\lambda(y-p),\qquad H_\lambda=(I_n+\lambda Q_d)^{-1}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, $(I_n+\lambda Q_d)x=y-p$ and $I_n+\lambda Q_d$ is positive definite for every finite $\lambda\ge0$. This affine expression is equivalent to the normal equations $(I_n+\lambda Q_d)\widehat\tau=y+\lambda\mu D_d^\top\mathbf1$.

\subsection{Null space, SVD, and limiting trends}
The rows of $D_d$ are independent in exact arithmetic, so $\operatorname{rank}(D_d)=n-d$ and $\dim\ker D_d=d$. The null space consists of the sampled polynomials of degree at most $d-1$. Let $D_d=U\Sigma V^\top$ be a full SVD. With nonnegative eigenvalues $\delta_j$ of $Q_d$, ordered so that exactly $d$ are zero, the smoother becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spectrum}
H_\lambda=V\diag\!\left(\frac{1}{1+\lambda\delta_1},\ldots,\frac{1}{1+\lambda\delta_n}\right)V^\top.
\end{equation}
Therefore the transformed observations $c=V^\top(y-p)$ can be computed once, and every new penalty value changes only the scalar attenuation factors $(1+\lambda\delta_j)^{-1}$. At $\lambda=\infty$ the exact limiting fit is $p+P_{\ker D_d}(y-p)$, rather than a computation using an arbitrarily large finite penalty.

For fixed $\mu$, the effective degrees of freedom are $\operatorname{EDF}(\lambda)=\tr(H_\lambda)=\sum_j(1+\lambda\delta_j)^{-1}$. We report Guerrero's trace-based smoothness $S_G=1-\tr(H_\lambda)/n$ alongside the normalized coordinate
\begin{equation}\label{eq:smoothness}
 S(\lambda)=\frac{n-\tr(H_\lambda)}{n-d}\in[0,1],\quad S(0)=0,\quad S(\infty)=1.
\end{equation}
For $1\le d<n$, this coordinate is strictly increasing for finite $\lambda$ because $S'(\lambda)=(n-d)^{-1}\sum_{\delta_j>0}\delta_j/(1+\lambda\delta_j)^2>0$. The normalization changes the scale of the original index but not the ordering of smoothness levels \cite{guerrero2008smoothness}.

\section{Classical parameter selection and numerical implementation}
\subsection{Selection criteria}
For a fixed numerical value of $\mu$, define $e_\lambda=y-\widehat\tau_{\lambda,\mu}$, $\operatorname{RSS}_\lambda=\|e_\lambda\|_2^2$, $h_{ii}=(H_\lambda)_{ii}$, and $\nu_\lambda=\tr(H_\lambda)$. The published classical selectors studied in \cite{cortes2017smoothness} are represented, up to constants irrelevant to an optimizer, by
\begin{align}\label{eq:criteria}
 \mathrm{CV}(\lambda)&=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\left(\frac{e_{\lambda,i}}{1-h_{ii}}\right)^2,\\
 \mathrm{GCV}(\lambda)&=\frac{\mathrm{RSS}_\lambda/n}{(1-\nu_\lambda/n)^2},\\
 \mathrm{AICc}(\lambda)&=\log(\mathrm{RSS}_\lambda/n)+\frac{2\nu_\lambda+1}{n-\nu_\lambda-2},\\
 \mathrm{BIC}(\lambda)&=\log(\mathrm{RSS}_\lambda/n)+\frac{\nu_\lambda\log n}{n}.
\end{align}
The software checks denominators and excludes undefined candidates, evaluates a declared positive grid of penalty values, and compares an exact infinite-penalty candidate. In particular, a near-interpolating BIC minimum can be driven by the $\log(\mathrm{RSS})$ boundary, as our example illustrates. We do not interpret a finite-grid BIC minimum close to zero as a universally reliable statistical choice.

Guerrero \cite{guerrero2007penalized} also considers the feasible plug-in $\widehat\mu=\operatorname{mean}(D_dy)$. This option is available \emph{for fitting}; however, the formula for leave-one-out CV in \eqref{eq:criteria} presumes that $\mu$ remains fixed under deletion. The current public selectors intentionally require a fixed numerical $\mu$. Likewise, $\tr(H_\lambda)$ is a conditional EDF and is not automatically the full degrees of freedom of a procedure that estimates $\mu$ from the same observations.

\subsection{Reusable computation and cost}
The package exposes a reusable \texttt{TrendSmoother(n, order=d)} object. Construction forms the difference operator and its SVD. For each candidate $\lambda$, a fitted curve is obtained with the transformed vector $c$ and spectral attenuation in \eqref{eq:spectrum}. The library offers \texttt{fit}, \texttt{degrees\_of\_freedom}, \texttt{smoothness}, and \texttt{select\_lambda}, while plotting utilities visualize fits and criterion profiles. Sphinx incorporates executable Jupyter notebooks, with deterministic random seeds and figure code for inspection.

The computational trade-off must distinguish decomposition from reuse. A dense full SVD costs $O(n^3)$ preprocessing and $O(n^2)$ memory; after projection, EDF, smoothness, GCV, AICc, and BIC summaries cost $O(n)$ per penalty candidate, while full reconstruction and exact CV leverage scores require $O(n^2)$ each. In contrast, the banded structure of $I_n+\lambda Q_d$ allows efficient specialized direct solvers \cite{weinert2007wh}. Thus the phrase \emph{fast computation} means repeated spectral evaluation under a stated workload, not a blanket claim that SVD beats banded factorization. A separate reproducible benchmark in the repository measures setup and fit times independently.

\section{Reproducible numerical illustration}
\subsection{Source-inspired nonlinear design}
To demonstrate the interface without introducing an external data source, we reproduce the nonlinear simulation family of Cort\'es-Toto et al. \cite{cortes2017smoothness}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:beta}
 \tau(t)=0.6f_{\mathrm{Beta}(30,17)}(t/n)+0.4f_{\mathrm{Beta}(3,11)}(t/n),\quad t=1,\ldots,n.
\end{equation}
We take $n=50$, add independent normal errors with standard deviation $0.5$, omit seasonality, and fix the pseudo-random seed at $19$. These choices define one illustrative replication, not a full reproduction of the article's factorial simulation. We fit $d=2$, $\mu=0$, and select $\lambda$ from the same 121-point geometric grid augmented with the exact $\lambda=\infty$ candidate under each criterion. The latent trend in \eqref{eq:beta} is known, allowing an \emph{ex post} trend-recovery RMSE; it is not a future forecast error.

\subsection{Algebraic and numerical checks}
An independent check compares the SVD implementation with the direct normal equations for $n=50$, $\lambda=30$, $d\in\{1,2,3\}$ and $\mu\in\{0,0.15\}$ on a second fixed-seed Gaussian sample. The maximum componentwise absolute difference is $2.01\times10^{-12}$ across these six cases, and the limiting constraint $D_d\widehat\tau_{\infty,\mu}=\mu\mathbf1$ holds to maximum error $4.42\times10^{-12}$. Both values result from the supplied deterministic regression script in IEEE double precision; they verify the tested algebra rather than establish stability for all $n,d,\lambda$. Higher differencing orders can be ill-conditioned, and systematic machine-dependent speed measurements remain a distinct stage of the study.

\section{Conclusions}
We have presented an auditable implementation of established penalized least-squares smoothing, together with additional derivations of the difference nullspace, the corrected smoothness index, and a Gaussian mean-difference profile estimator. The latter has numerical checks but is not yet a released joint-MLE API. Our Mauna Loa observations give a real, explicitly descriptive application of high-order differencing. Spectral decomposition makes repeated penalty evaluation transparent and inexpensive after preprocessing; specialized banded solvers remain important competitors for isolated fits and large samples. The source-inspired experiment shows how the same observations can lead to distinct smoothness choices under established criteria, while deterministic checks support correctness in a bounded set of cases. Broader performance conclusions require reported machine characteristics, workload-specific runtimes, and stability diagnostics. Neither high-order differencing nor likelihood-based smoothing is claimed as a newly discovered concept. No GPU acceleration has been verified in the released CPU implementation.
