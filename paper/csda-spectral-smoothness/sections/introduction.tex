\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
Finite-difference penalized least squares (PLS) estimates a trend by balancing fidelity to observations against a penalty on its discrete differences. This approach builds on classical Whittaker--Henderson smoothing \citep{whittaker1923,guerrero2007}. Although the penalty $\lambda$ is convenient for computation, the same value can imply different degrees of smoothing across sample sizes and difference orders. Guerrero's trace-based measure \citep{guerrero2008}, subsequently studied alongside classical selection criteria by \citet{cortes2017}, provides a way to describe the fitted trend in terms of effective degrees of freedom.

The quadratic second-difference filter underlying familiar macroeconomic trend--cycle decomposition \citep{hodrickprescott1997} is a particular member of this family. Related but distinct regularizers include difference penalties on B-spline coefficients in P-splines \citep{eilersmarx1996} and $\ell_1$ trend filtering, which promotes piecewise-linear fits with localized changes in slope \citep{kim2009}. These comparisons delimit the present scope: we study the quadratic observation-grid difference penalty, not every notion of trend smoothness.

Our focus is the bounded coordinate $s=S_d(\lambda)\in[0,1]$. Its endpoints represent interpolation and the constrained polynomial limit, respectively. The limiting polynomial has degree at most $d-1$ for $\mu=0$ and can have degree $d$ for fixed nonzero $\mu$. This is an exact change of coordinates for a published estimator, not a new estimator, loss function or guarantee of better optimization. A user can request $s$ while the numerical calculation internally evaluates $\lambda=S_d^{-1}(s)$.

Efficient algorithms for Whittaker--Henderson and spline smoothing are well established. \citet{weinert2007} develops specialized structural and GCV computations; related approaches exploit tensor-product structure and frequency-domain transformations \citep{eilers2006,garcia2010}. \citet{biessy2026} places Whittaker--Henderson smoothing in a modern statistical framework with Bayesian and computational extensions. Dense SVD cannot be characterized as universally faster than these structured methods. We examine whether a consistent spectral representation can improve the reliability of smoothness inversion, trend reconstruction and criterion evaluation, particularly at high difference orders and near the smoothing endpoints.

The statistical selection literature is equally established. The GCV construction for smoothing splines \citep{craven1979} and its formulation for ridge regularization \citep{golub1979} predate this work. Smoothness-prior time-series models also provide alternative likelihood and state-space interpretations of smooth trend estimation \citep{kitagawa2003}. Our spectral presentation is consequently an implementation and numerical-validity contribution rather than the invention of cross-validation, smoothness priors, or regularization selection.

For nonzero reference differences, the usual affine formula constructs a polynomial of degree $d$. Its entries can grow large relative to the observations, causing cancellation and loss of floating-point accuracy. We derive a shift-free expression from the singular vectors of the difference operator that avoids this intermediate. It is tested against an independent 85-digit reference, with numerical failures retained in the analysis rather than silently discarded.

A separate issue concerns the residual-based BIC expression used by \citet{cortes2017}. For nondegenerate data, the residual sum of squares is of order $\lambda^2$ near interpolation, so this expression tends to $-\infty$ as $\lambda\downarrow0$. We prove the limit, record it as a limitation of the unrestricted criterion and evaluate two explicitly constrained candidate ranges. Finally, a small simulation compares established criteria using the true latent trend, not a fitted residual or a future forecast.

We contribute (i) a unified smoothness-indexed SVD formulation and a shift-free identity for fitting; (ii) a numerical-validation study with independent multiprecision reference solutions; (iii) an explicit analysis of the BIC interpolation boundary; and (iv) reproducible pilot evidence on true trend-recovery error. Full spectral SVD is adopted as an accuracy-oriented default for the proposed framework, while its dense computational cost and remaining prior-art benchmarks are acknowledged.
